Chapter 1: The Last Prisoner

The cell smelled of wet straw and old iron. Kael sat with his back against the wall and counted the drips from the ceiling, one per breath, the way his father had taught him to count the days of a siege. The drip stopped at forty-one, and the door opened.

Two men in grey coats came in and did not look at him. One carried a ledger, the other a length of rope. The one with the ledger read the charge in a flat voice, the way a clerk reads a tax list.

"Kael of the Dawnward Order. Theft from a Coven granary. One sack of barley, one measure of salt, one blanket marked with the seal of the Iron Coven."

"It was winter," Kael said. "The granary was full."

The clerk looked up for the first time. "That is not in the record."

"Put it in the record."

The clerk closed the ledger. "Your order is dissolved. Your name is struck. You are sentenced to the Long March. The sentence begins at dawn."

They left the rope. A man sentenced to the Long March could walk or be dragged; the rope was a courtesy. He left it on the floor.

At dawn they marched him through the West Gate. The Ashfall had been falling for ninety years. The Coven said it would stop when the old oaths were forgotten; Kael had heard that lie so often it sounded like a weather report.

The March formed in the mud outside the gate: forty prisoners, two wardens, one cart. The wardens wore iron masks, smooth and featureless, so that no prisoner could say he had seen a warden's face and lived.

Kael found a place at the back of the line, ahead of an old soldier with a gash across his cheek and a cough that shook his frame: Roran, a knight of the Dawnward Order twenty years before Kael was born.

"They struck my name in a different season," Roran said, wiping his mouth. "The record keeps no weather."

On the third day, a prisoner died, a boy no older than eighteen, walking on a foot that had turned black. The wardens ordered the line to keep moving. Kael stopped.

He did not plan it. He simply stopped, bent down, and gave the dead boy the blanket off his own back: the same blanket he had stolen from the Coven granary, marked with their seal.

"Cover his face," Kael said to no one in particular.

A warden walked over, the mask turning toward him, and for a moment Kael thought the man would strike him down. Instead the warden looked at the blanket, at the seal, and then at Kael.

"You stole that blanket to cover a corpse," the warden said. "That is the most honest theft I have ever recorded."

The line moved on, and the ash settled on Kael's shoulders like a second coat.

He did not see the girl in the grey hood at the road's edge: a clerk of the Coven archive, on her way to the coast with a satchel of records meant to be burned, who had watched the March for three days and seen one man stop.

She wrote a single line in the private ledger hidden in her coat's lining.

"Kael of the Dawnward Order. Believed dead. Last seen covering a corpse with a stolen blanket. Note to self: find out what kind of man steals a blanket twice."

Chapter 2: The Long March

Kael walked beside Roran because Roran coughed when he talked and talked when he coughed, and the sound was better than silence.

That night the wardens lit no fire. The prisoners huddled in the dark, and the ash fell like slow snow. Kael woke to a voice, thin and clear, speaking a single word.

"Oath."

He sat up. The camp was still. The voice had come from inside his head, or from the ground, or from between the two.

Roran was watching him from the dark.

"You heard it too," Roran said. It was not a question.

"The curse is not a punishment, boy. It is a debt. Ninety years of unkept oaths, and the land is still calling them in."

Kael looked at his hands. In the dark they seemed to give off a faint silver light, no brighter than a struck match, gone before he could be sure.

"Don't tell the wardens," Roran said. "There's a word for men who light up in the dark. The Coven pays well for them."

"What word?"

Roran did not answer. He lay back down, and the ash fell on them both like snow on statues.

On the seventh day, the girl in the grey hood caught up with the March.

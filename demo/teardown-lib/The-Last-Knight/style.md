# Style Analysis: The Last Knight

## Voice Summary

Dry, concrete, and economical. The narration trusts the reader: it states facts and lets subtext do the emotional work. The closest single description is "a clerk reading a tax list" - the book's own image for authority, used so often that it becomes the book's signature.

## Sentence Rhythm

Deterministic statistics on the 6,200-word sample (chapters 1-5):

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Average sentence length | 12.4 words |
| Median sentence length | 11 words |
| Short sentences (1-6 words) | 27% |
| Medium (7-18 words) | 55% |
| Long (19+ words) | 18% |
| Paragraphs of one sentence | 41% |

The rhythm is a deliberate walking rhythm: short declarative sentences with occasional longer ones used only for world description or a character's inner accounting. The sample rarely runs two long sentences in a row; a long sentence is always followed by a short one that lands the point.

## Sentence-Level Anchors from the Source

1. "The drip stopped at forty-one, and the door opened." - the short second clause performs the chapter's first beat change.
2. "They left the rope." - four words, full paragraph, maximum implication.
3. "The record keeps no weather." - the old knight's proverb; compression as characterization.
4. "Note to self: find out what kind of man steals a blanket twice." - internal monologue that sounds like an archive entry, because the POV character is an archivist.

## Punctuation Habits

- Periods dominate. No exclamation marks in the sample outside dialogue.
- Em-dashes appear once per chapter at most, always as a single deliberate pivot, never in clusters.
- Semicolons are used for the narrator's accounting voice ("forty prisoners, two Coven wardens, and a cart with three wheels" is a list, not a semicolon sentence; the semicolon appears when the narrator tallies consequences).
- Dialogue tags are almost always "said", "said no one in particular", "asked". No adverbial tags.

## Dialogue and Subtext

Dialogue does two jobs: it states the theme (Roran's "the land remembers what we feed it") and it advances the debt chain ("Put it in the record"). Characters rarely say what they feel. The warden's compliment is the emotional peak of chapter 1, and it is delivered as a bureaucratic observation.

Subtext rule observed: when a character cares, they change the subject or the ritual. Kael's care for the dead boy is shown by stopping, not by speaking.

## Emotion Pacing

Emotional beats are placed on a "debt schedule": the book gives the reader one moment of warmth per chapter, always attached to an act of integrity, and one moment of chill, always attached to the curse. The warm and cold beats never share a paragraph. Chapter 3's warmth (Maren shares her bread) is immediately followed by the Vowless's arrival, and the contrast is the chapter's engine.

## How to Use This File

Before writing a scene in this benchmark's voice: read the rhythm stats, then imitate the anchor sentences. Keep paragraphs short. Let one sentence carry the pivot. Do not explain emotion; let the weather, the ritual, or the ledger do it.

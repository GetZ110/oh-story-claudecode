THE SECRET KEEPER
(Excerpt from the source short story, ~6,000 words total; this backup holds the analyzed sample)

---

The letter arrived on the tenth of November, the same day every year the sea gave something back.

Elara found it wedged under the lighthouse door, the paper damp and the ink blurred at the edges, as if it had swum the last mile itself. She did not need to open it to know the handwriting. Five years of widowhood had not taught her to forget the way Callum leaned on the tails of his t's.

She carried it up the stairs anyway. The lamp room smelled of oil and salt and her own tired routine, and she sat in the chair where she had sat on the day of his funeral, and she opened the letter.

"Elara. I am alive. I know what you must think, and you are right to think it. The debt collectors found me in the autumn of that year, and I did what the sea does best. I disappeared. I let them believe the water took me. I let you believe it too, because they told me they would go to you next, and I thought that if you grieved, they would not dig. I have spent five years in the north, mending nets and learning to be nobody. It is a smaller life than the one we planned, but it is a life.

They are gone now. The last of them died in prison this spring, and I have been watching the papers to be certain. I am coming home. The deed to the lighthouse is in your name, as you know, and I do not want it back. I want the chair by the fire and the smell of lamp oil and the sound of you telling me I am a fool.

Do not tell anyone I am coming. Not the priest, not the grocer, not your sister. You are the only one who has ever kept a secret for me, and I am asking you to keep this one a little longer. I will be on the evening boat on the last day of the month. I will be the one who does not look like a man returning from the dead.

Callum."

Elara read it three times. Then she folded it along the old creases, the ones the paper had learned in five years of waiting to be read, and she put it in the tin box where she kept the letters of the dead. The tin box had held the death certificate, the condolence cards, and the salvage company's letter confirming that a man's coat had been found on the rocks. It had never held anything alive before.

She went back down to the kitchen and made tea. Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She had imagined this moment, the way widows do, in the dark hours when the lamp beam swept the sea and found nothing. In her imaginings she had wept, or shouted, or thrown the letter into the stove. In reality she drank the tea, and the tea tasted the way the house had tasted for five years, which is to say it tasted of nothing.

The evening boat came on the last day of the month. She stood on the pier with her coat buttoned to the chin, and she watched the passengers come down the gangplank. There was a man in a grey cap who walked with the careful stride of a man who has learned to be nobody. He walked past her, past the end of the pier, and then stopped, because the pier ended and the sea began.

"Callum," she said.

He turned. He was thinner than the man in the photographs. His face had the patient, empty look of a man who has spent five years not being himself. He opened his mouth, and she saw that he had rehearsed something, and that the rehearsing had not survived the journey.

"I kept your secret," she said. "I kept it for five years without knowing I was keeping it. That has to count for something."

"It counts for everything," he said. "It's the only thing I have."

They walked back to the lighthouse together. The lamp was already burning; it burned every night, because the sea did not care whether a widow was grieving or a husband was returning. Elara watched him stop at the door and touch the frame the way a man touches the frame of a house he has not allowed himself to remember.

"I have to tell you something," he said.

"You can tell me anything. I have kept worse secrets than yours, and I did not even know them."

"Not that," he said. "The boat. There was a woman on the boat. She watched me the whole crossing. She knew my face, Elara. She called me by name before I could stop her."

"Who was she?"

"She said she was the widow of the man who went to prison for my debts. She said the sea had a way of balancing its books."

The lamp beam swept the water below them, and for a moment the light caught a small boat, riding low in the swell, with a single figure at the oars, who was not rowing toward the shore.

Elara closed the door.

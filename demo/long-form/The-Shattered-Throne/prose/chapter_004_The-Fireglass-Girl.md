# The Fireglass Girl

The glassyard stood at the edge of the district, where the cart road met the river and the ash piled deep against the kilns' walls. The kilns had been cold for years, their glass carried off piece by piece until only the bones remained: broken arches, rusted bellows, shelves of dust.

Sera was waiting inside, sitting on a stack of dead firebrick with a piece of fireglass in her hand.

"You came," she said.

"You said you were leaving," Corin said. "People who are leaving don't leave notes telling people where to find them."

The note had been chalked on the lamp room door, in a hand that was not Mila's: "Glassyard, dusk, the one who sells light."

Sera turned the fireglass in her fingers. The light inside it moved with her hand the way water moves in a cup, slow and heavy and alive.

"Here's the truth," she said. "The fireglass comes from the barons' kilns. It's the quarter's luxury. They tax the district in bread and oil, and they sell the district its light back in glass, and they call it trade."

"And you sell it under them."

"I steal it from them." She said it plainly, without heat. "The kilns are in my father's quarter. I know the stores, the schedules, the guards' habits. I've been taking glass for a year, and I've been selling it here, and I've been putting the coin away."

"For what?"

"For leaving." She looked at him, and for the first time the calculation in her face went out, and something younger looked out of it. "I'm Baron Halvath's daughter. My father's name is worth something in the quarter, and nothing anywhere else. The only thing it's good for is being called in. He has a claim on me. The kind of claim that comes with a price on my head if I stop being useful."

The book hummed at the edge of Corin's sight.

He did not want it to open. It opened anyway.

The page was Sera's. He knew it before he read it, the way he knew the oil count at the end of a hard month. The entry was old, and the hand was the baron's own.

"Debt: Sera Blackwood owes her father one obedience. Uncollected. Collector: none."

Corin read it, and Sera watched him read it, and the glassyard went very quiet.

"You can see it," she said. "The book. You can see my page."

"Yes."

"Then you know what it says."

"Yes."

She stood up, and the fireglass in her hand burned brighter.

"My father's claim is the reason I can't run. If I leave, he calls the debt, and the book pays him, and I'm bound to come back. I've been paying it off in glass, piece by piece. But it compounds, Corin. It compounds like everything in this realm."

The book turned a page.

"Collection offered," it wrote, in ink that burned. "Collect the debt of Sera Blackwood. She will be bound to the collector's protection. The bond will hold until the debt is settled in full."

Corin read the offer. The arithmetic was simple, and the arithmetic was the trap. If he collected her debt, she would be his to protect, bound by the book, and the baron's claim would be struck through, and her father could never call her home again.

He looked at Sera and saw what the offer would cost her. Not the binding, which was the point of it. The cost was that she would owe him. She had spent her whole life owing her father, and the book was offering to make her his ledger entry the way she was her father's.

"No," he said.

The book's page went still.

"Refusal," it wrote. "The collector refuses the collection. The debt remains open. The refusal is settled in memory."

The page burned.

It burned the way the Tallow Gate lantern had burned when he lit it: cleanly, completely, with a light that was not light. And when it went out, there was a hole in Corin's head where a word had been. He knew the shape of the word, the way you know the shape of a tooth you have lost, and he could not say it, and he could not remember whose name it was.

He stood in the glassyard with the hole in his head, and he did not tell Sera. He smiled, or he tried to, and the smile must have been wrong, because she took a step toward him.

"What happened?"

"The book is exact," he said. "That's all."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she took his hand, turned it over, and looked at the brand on his arm: the two pages, and the third, the refusal's burn, still fading at the edge.

"Refusal," she read. "You refused the collection."

"I refused the collection."

"Why?"

"Because you didn't owe me." He pulled his hand back, carefully. "You've owed your father your whole life. You came here to owe no one. I'm not going to be the next entry."

The glassyard was quiet. The river ran past outside, carrying ash, and Sera Blackwood stood in the ruin of her family's trade and looked at the lamplighter's apprentice who had just paid a memory to let her keep owing no one.

"You're a fool," she said.

"I've been called worse."

"Not by me." She sat back down on the firebrick, and the calculation came back into her face, but it was a different calculation now. "The Ash Festival is in six nights. The quarter empties out. The guards drink, the gates open, and the river takes anything that floats. I'm leaving on the festival night, and I'm taking a year of glass with me."

"Then you're leaving," Corin said.

"Yes." She looked at him. "And if you want to come with me, the boat holds two."

He did not answer. The hole in his head was still warm, and he could feel the shape of the missing word, and he knew, with the same certainty he knew the oil count and the lamp tax, that the word was his mother's name, and that it would not come back.

Mila was waiting for him at the top of the glassyard road, sitting on the wall with her chalk and her loaf.

"You look like someone took something from you," she said.

"Someone did."

"Was it worth it?"

He looked back at the glassyard, where the baron's daughter sat in the ruin of her family's trade, counting the nights until she was free.

"I don't know yet," he said. "Ask me in six nights."

They walked home through the dusk, the lamps coming on around them, steady against the wind. In the barons' quarter, a captain of the Ash Guard listened to a glassyard worker's description of a boy and a baron's daughter, smiled with his teeth, and sent word to the quarter's oldest house.

The word arrived at Baron Halvath's table during the evening meal. He read it twice, burned it once, and went on eating his bread as if the ash had not just learned his daughter's name.

# The Ledger Speaks

The book had no pages for the living.

Corin learned this at dawn, sitting on his cot with the window open and the ash settling on the sill. When he thought of the book, it opened. When he thought of it closed, it closed. It sat in the corner of his vision like a lamp that had been burning all night and had decided to keep burning.

He counted what he saw. The first page held his own entry, the debt the realm owed him for one light. The second page was the strange line about the throne's debt, uncollected, with no collector. The pages after that were blank, and the blankness was not empty. It hummed, faintly, like a held note.

"Tomas Hatch," he read aloud, and the book opened itself to a page that had not been there a moment before.

The entry was old. The ink had browned at the edges, and the hand was the steady, practiced hand of a man who had written the same sentence every night for years.

"Oath sworn: Tomas Hatch, lamplighter, will light the Tallow Gate lantern each night until the flame returns. Unkept: two hundred nights. Debt owed: the light. Collector: none."

The Tallow Gate was the north gate, where the old cart road came in from the ash. Corin had walked past it a thousand times and never once seen the lantern above it lit, because no one remembered a man named Tomas Hatch.

Pell knew the name.

He was sitting in the kitchen with his tea when Corin asked, and the old man's hand went still on the cup.

"Tomas Hatch," Pell said. "He was the gate lamplighter. Before your time. Before mine, mostly. He lit that lantern every night for forty years, and he said he would keep lighting it until the flame returned."

"And?"

"And the flame did not return." Pell drank his tea. "The barons stopped paying for the oil. He lit it anyway, out of his own wages, until the wages ran out. Then he lit it with candles. Then he died, and the lantern went dark, and nobody lit it again."

"Two hundred nights," Corin said.

"Two hundred nights, give or take. He kept the count himself, on the gate post, in chalk." Pell looked at him. "Why do you want to know about Tomas Hatch?"

Corin thought about the book, and the hum of the blank pages.

"No reason," he said. "I heard the name in the market."

Pell let the lie pass. The old man had a way of letting lies pass that was worse than asking, because it meant he was keeping his own count.

Corin went to the Tallow Gate at noon.

The gate was a tall arch of black stone, worn smooth by a hundred years of ash and weather. The lantern above it hung from an iron arm, rusted, empty, its glass filmed grey. On the gate post, faint and almost gone, were chalk marks: two hundred tally marks, a gap, and the beginning of a third hundred.

Tomas Hatch had counted to two hundred and one before he stopped.

The ash guards came through the gate at that moment, three of them in grey, walking the noon patrol. One of them looked at Corin standing under the gate and said nothing, and that was the district's idea of a friendly patrol.

Corin waited until they were gone. Then he climbed the gate.

The stone was slick with ash. He found handholds the way the district's children found them, by feel and by memory, and pulled himself onto the arch. The lantern hung at the center, rusted, filmed grey, its glass cleaned one last time by a man who knew he was dying.

There was a candle inside. Stub of a candle, burned down to a thumb, left behind in the lantern's socket by someone who had meant to come back.

Corin held his hand to it. The warmth under his sleeve, the page that had burned into his arm the night before, went out along his fingers like a wick drawing flame.

The stub lit.

The light filled the lantern, and the glass, filmed grey for years, came clear, and the Tallow Gate lantern burned at noon over a street that had forgotten it.

In the corner of his vision, the book opened. The page for Tomas Hatch turned, and the entry, the two hundred nights of unkept promise, the debt with no collector, struck itself through with a single line of silver ink.

The page went blank, and the blankness was warm.

The book turned to a new leaf, and wrote:

"Collection paid. Title granted: Keeper of the Lanterns. Flame does not gutter near the bearer. Due: one lamp kept burning, each night, in the bearer's keeping."

The words burned themselves onto Corin's forearm, under his sleeve, in ink he could feel but not see. He slid down from the gate, and the district's noon went about its business underneath, and nobody looked up, because nobody remembered the lantern well enough to notice it had come back.

Mila was waiting for him at the end of the street.

She had the chalk in her fist and the loaf under her coat, and she looked at the gate, then at him.

"It's lit," she said. "The dead man's lamp. You lit it."

"I lit it," Corin said.

"Does the book pay in coin?"

The question was so practical, and so exactly Mila, that he almost laughed.

"It pays in titles," he said.

"Titles," she said, testing the word. "Can you eat a title?"

"I don't know yet."

She thought about this, seriously, the way she thought about everything.

"Then you should learn fast," she said. "The dead man's lamp is lit, and the ash guards count everything that changes."

She was gone before he could answer, slipping between the houses, and the lantern burned above the gate, steady against the noon wind.

Back in the lamp room, with the door closed, Corin tested the title.

He lit a lamp, carried it to the window, and opened the casement. The wind came off the river, hard and cold, and the flame leaned, and then it stood. He blew on it, softly, and it did not move. He closed his fist around it, and the flame burned against his palm without heat, and when he opened his hand it stood in the air above his skin.

The book opened in the corner of his vision, far back, in the oldest part of the ledger, where the pages were stiff and the ink was brown. It opened to a single page, and on that page, in a hand that looked like a king's, one line stood alone:

"The flame is a promise. Guard it."

The book closed itself, and the hum of the blank pages went quiet, and the district's lamps burned down Flame Street like old soldiers who had remembered their orders.

Mila was right about one thing. The ash guards counted everything that changed. And tonight, the street was one lamp longer than the records said it could be.

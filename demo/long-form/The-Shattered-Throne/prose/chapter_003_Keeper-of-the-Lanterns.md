# Keeper of the Lanterns

The fireglass arrived on a market day, and the district did not know what to do with it.

The woman who sold it set up between the fish cart and the cobbler, laying out wares on black cloth: pieces of glass the size of a hand, clear as river ice, with something burning inside them. Not a flame, and not a coal. A light that sat in the glass the way a held breath sits in a chest.

"It burns without oil," she said to the crowd. "It burns in the wind. It burns for a month on one lighting. Buy it, or ask your lamplighter why his street goes dark at midnight."

The district laughed at the price, and then it stopped laughing, because the woman held a piece of the glass up against the wind, and the light inside it did not so much as lean.

Pell studied her from the edge of the crowd with the careful, wounded look of a man watching his trade be replaced.

"Fireglass," he said to Corin. "The barons' kilns make it. It costs more than oil, but it never runs out, and the barons control it, and the barons are always generous with what they can take back."

"She's from the quarter," Corin said.

"Everyone's from somewhere, boy."

The woman was young, with a coat that had once been good, and she caught Corin watching her and smiled, a calculation wearing a friendly face.

"Lamplighter's apprentice," she said. "You'd be the one who lit the dead man's lantern."

The market went quiet around them, the way the district went quiet when something changed.

"I don't know any dead men," Corin said.

"You lit the Tallow Gate at noon, two days ago. The whole gate heard about it before the guards did." She held up the fireglass. "I'm Sera. I sell light the barons don't want you to have. You make light the barons can't explain. We should talk."

Pell put his hand on Corin's shoulder.

"We don't buy from the quarter," he said. "The quarter sells, and then it collects."

"I'm not collecting," Sera said. "I'm leaving."

She said it lightly, but the district heard the weight under it, and Pell's hand tightened, then let go.

"Light your lamps, boy," Pell said, and walked back to the lamp room.

The afternoon went badly.

Corin tried to test the title in private, behind the lamp room, where the wind came down the alley between two walls of grey stone. He lit a lamp and set it on a barrel; the flame stood in the wind like a painted flame, the ash falling past it. He was watching the arithmetic of it when the children found him.

The children always found him. He counted the oil and knew the bread carts, and they treated him the way the district treated the old lamps: as furniture that was also a promise.

A gust came down the alley, and the flame leaned, and then it stood. A second gust came, harder, and it leaned again, and stood again, and the oldest of the children, a boy with a scrape on his knee, pointed and said the words that ended Corin's quiet life:

"It won't gutter. Look. It won't gutter."

The word traveled. By dusk, the district had a new name for its lamplighter's apprentice.

Mila found him at the foot of Flame Street, where the wind came hardest.

"Bad news," she said. "The merchant with the glass caught me. My fault. I took the cobbler's bread, and now I owe the merchant, and he wants a name."

"A name?"

"He says I owe him a name." For the first time since he had known her, she looked small. "A name of someone who owes something. The quarter pays for names."

The merchant had found him. Corin understood it in the same instant he understood the fireglass, and the woman who sold it, and the market's quiet. The quarter did not sell light. It bought names. And the district had given it one.

"Stay here," Corin said.

"Where are you going?"

"To owe him a name instead."

The merchant's stall was still up when the dusk lamps came on. He was a wide man with the quarter's manners and the district's eyes, counting his day's take when Corin walked up.

"You want a name," Corin said.

"I want a name," the merchant said. "The girl took from me. The district takes from me. The quarter tells me I take from them. I need a name that clears my books."

Corin looked at him, and then he did the thing he had been practicing all afternoon, the thing the title had taught him. He lit the lamp at the corner of the merchant's stall, in the full wind, and the flame stood.

The merchant's eyes went to it, and stayed there.

"That's the name," Corin said. "The boy whose lamp will not gutter. Sell it to the quarter, if you like. But tell them the name comes with the light, and the light comes with the debt, and the debt has a collector now."

The merchant looked at the lamp, at the flame standing in the wind, at the boy who had made it.

He said nothing. He was, for the first time in his life, doing the arithmetic, and the sum frightened him.

Corin walked back up the street. The dusk lamps were coming on, one by one, and the wind was rising, and the flames stood along Flame Street like a line of held breaths.

An old man in a patched coat waited at the top of the street. He had been watching the lamps come on, and the boy who lit them, and when Corin reached him he put out a hand with a tremor in it and took him by the elbow, the way a blind man takes a guide.

"Page-Bearer," the old man said.

The word was old, older than the barons, older than the ash: a word from the time when the flame had burned and the throne had been a promise.

"Who are you?" Corin said.

"My name is Edric," the old man said. "I was the steward of the court that died. And they told me you were a myth."

He looked at the brand on Corin's arm, visible now in the lamp light, two pages of dark ink under the sleeve.

"They were wrong," he said. "You're a boy with a book, and the book is the throne, and the throne has been waiting for you for a hundred years."

Down in the square, the fireglass woman packed her stall and counted her day's take. She had sold four pieces of glass and learned one name, and the name was worth more than the glass.

She wrote the name on a scrap of paper, added a second line under it that she would never send, and tucked both into the lining of her coat.

In the barons' quarter, a captain of the Ash Guard listened to his sergeant's report about a boy whose lamp would not gutter.

"Find him," Rook said. "The barons have a price for steady light."

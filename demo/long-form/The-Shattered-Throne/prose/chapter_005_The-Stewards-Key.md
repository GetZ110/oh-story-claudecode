# The Steward's Key

Edric told the story in the lamp room, with the door barred and the lamps burning low, because the story was the kind that the barons had made illegal a hundred years ago and the district had learned not to hear.

"The First Flame was not a fire," he said. "That's the whole of it, and it's the only part the barons never understood."

Corin sat on the oil chest. Mila sat on the floor with her chalk, drawing sums she did not need, the way she listened.

"Every kingdom has a founding story," Edric went on. "A king who slew a beast, a queen who bargained with the sea. Ours was different. Our first king swore an oath: the flame would burn as long as the realm kept its promises. The realm kept its promises, the flame burned, and the throne was a lamp, the promise made visible."

"The throne was a lamp," Corin repeated.

"You've seen it in the old carvings. A lamp above a chair. The lamp was the flame, the chair the promise it stood on. They were the same thing, and the barons have spent a hundred years pretending they were two."

Mila looked up from her chalk.

"What happened to it?"

"The last king died, and the flame went out with him." Edric's voice was careful, the voice of a man walking on old ice. "The barons' ancestor did not extinguish a fire. He buried a promise. He carried the lamp under his own hall and sealed it in a chapel with a debt only royal blood could pay, so the flame could not return and the realm could not remember what it had lost."

"How do you know all this?" Corin said.

"I was the steward of the court that died." Edric reached into his coat and brought out a key, too long and thin, sized like nothing Corin had ever locked. "I have kept the key to the chapel for forty years, waiting for a Page-Bearer who was supposed to be a myth."

The key was warm. It had been warm for forty years, waiting for the boy who could see the book.

"It's real," Corin said.

"It's real. And so is the chapel, and so is the flame, and so is the debt that seals it." Edric's voice dropped. "And so is the thing that has been looking for you since the market. The Ash Guard has a new captain. His name is Rook, and he has been asking about a boy whose lamp will not gutter."

The knock came at the lamp room door.

It was not a knock. It was a fist, and it came twice, and then the door came off its hinges.

Rook filled the doorway, a tall man with the quarter's manners and the Ash Guard's reach, holding the door's hinge in one hand the way other men held a cup. Behind him, the street was full of grey coats.

"The boy whose lamp will not gutter," Rook said. "The barons have a price for steady light, and for the steward with the key. Interesting, that you found each other."

Mila was already moving. She went out the back window before Corin could tell her to, and the street outside began, quietly, to empty.

"Walk," Rook said.

They walked. The ash guard herded them down Flame Street past the lamps Corin had lit and the houses gone dark and still. The district's doors stayed shut, and the lamps burned on in the wind, the only things in the district that did not know how to fear.

The Tallow Gate stood at the street's end. Above it, Tomas Hatch's lantern burned, lit at noon, burning against everything.

Rook stopped under the gate.

"That lamp," he said. "It was dead for years. I used to climb this gate as a boy and watch the old man light it with candles, and I thought it was the stupidest thing I had ever seen." He looked at the lantern, and his voice changed, just slightly. "A lamp that won't die, lit by a boy who won't stay dark. The barons are afraid of it."

"They should be," Edric said.

Rook turned. His hand came up, and the ash guard behind him drew steel, and the street was very still.

"Give me the key," Rook said. "And the boy. And nobody needs to bleed on the stones tonight."

Corin looked at the lantern above the gate, burning steady in the wind, and understood, with the clarity of a sum finally adding up, what the title was for.

He stepped forward.

"You can have the key," he said. "It won't open anything for you. The chapel's seal is a debt, and only royal blood can pay it, and the book keeps the accounts."

"The book," Rook said. "The book is a story the barons tell to keep the district quiet."

"The book is real." Corin reached up, his hand closing on the lantern's chain; the flame above him did not so much as lean. "I lit this lamp at noon. I lit forty-two lamps with one candle. The book records debts and pays titles, and it does not care who believes in it. It only cares who pays."

The wind came down the street.

It came hard, the way the river wind came at dusk, and every torch in the ash guards' hands leaned and guttered, and the lamps along Flame Street stood, and the lantern above the gate stood, and the light fell on Rook's face and held it.

Rook looked at the boy under the lantern, at the key in the steward's hand, at the street that would not go dark.

"Bring me the boy," he told his guards.

The guards did not move.

"Bring him."

Still they did not move. They stood in the light of a hundred lamps that would not gutter, and the wind came again, and their torches guttered, and the lamps stood.

Rook looked at them, and then at Corin, and for a long moment the captain and the lamplighter stood under the Tallow Gate with the dead man's lantern burning between them.

"Collect your lamps," Rook said at last. "And collect your steward. And understand that the barons do not lose twice in one week."

He turned and walked back down the street, his guards following, their torches going out one by one in the wind, the lamps of Flame Street standing steady above them to the district's edge.

Mila was waiting at the end of the street, in the dark, with her chalk and her loaf.

"He'll be back," she said.

"He'll be back," Corin agreed.

The book opened in the corner of his vision, quiet, patient, and turned itself to the front page. A line wrote itself in the oldest ink, in the hand of the first king, addressed to no one living.

"Entry: the Debt of the Throne. Collector: the Page-Bearer. Due: when the flame returns."

Corin read it, and the key in his hand was warm, and the lantern burned above the gate, and the ash fell on the district like the slow breathing of something that had been waiting a hundred years to be woken.

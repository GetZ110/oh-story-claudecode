# A Last Candle

The lamp tax was due at midnight, and Corin had one candle left.

He held it in his fist like a piece of the sky and tried to make the math work. Forty-two lamps on Flame Street. One candle. A wind coming off the river that had eaten every flame the district had lit this month. The numbers did not add up, and numbers always added up. That was the one thing Pell had taught him that the city could not tax.

"You count bread," Pell said from the doorway of the lamp room. "You count oil. You count everything except your own luck, boy."

"I counted the luck," Corin said. "It ran out in the third week of the ash."

The ash was falling again. It fell the way rain fell in other cities, grey and constant and taken for granted. It coated the gutters, piled against the doorsteps, and tasted of salt when the wind carried it off the river. The barons said the ash was the realm's judgment on the old king's broken oath. Corin had heard that so many times it sounded like a weather report.

"One candle, forty-two lamps," Pell said. "What does the arithmetic say?"

"It says the tax man collects at midnight and the street goes dark."

"So we let it go dark."

Corin looked at the street. The lamps stood along Flame Street like a row of old soldiers, empty glass and cold wicks, waiting for an oil that did not exist. Every house on the street had paid its share of the tax in bread and copper. The barons' collectors had taken the copper and left the bread. That was how the district worked: the ash fell, the tax came, and the lamps died one by one until the street gave up on them.

"Not tonight," Corin said.

He went out with the candle. Pell watched him go and said nothing, because the old man had learned that Corin's arithmetic had two columns: the one that counted oil, and the one that counted pride.

The first lamp took a quarter of the candle's flame. The wind took it before he could close the glass. He cupped the light, walked to the second lamp, and the wind took that one too. The third flame he sheltered against his chest with both hands, and it burned low and stubborn, and he walked the length of the street with it, feeding each lamp from the last one's fire.

The street woke behind him. Windows opened. Old women leaned out and watched the light travel, lamp by lamp, like a slow tide coming home. The children came to the doors. Someone started counting aloud with him, and then someone else, until the whole street counted in his wake.

Forty-one lamps. The candle was down to a stub, and the wind was rising.

The last lamp stood at the end of the street, in front of the house with the boarded windows. The house had been empty since the ash winter took its family. The tax records said the street ended there, and the street had agreed for three years. The last lamp had not been lit since the day the family left.

Corin looked at it. The stub burned in his palm.

"It's dead," a voice said from the shadows of a broken cart. "Everyone says so. The street ends there."

The voice belonged to a girl, small and quick, with a piece of chalk in her fist. She watched him the way the district's cats watched the tax collectors: carefully, without blinking, ready to be gone.

"The street ends where the lamp goes out," Corin said. "Not before."

He walked to the last lamp, opened the glass, and fed it the stub's final flame. The wick caught, sputtered, and held. The wind came up the street, hard and cold, and every flame behind him leaned and wavered, and the last flame leaned too, and then it stood. It stood straight and still in the wind like a soldier who had been ordered not to move.

The girl stared at it.

"That's not possible," she said.

"It's arithmetic," Corin said, though he did not believe it.

Behind them, the district's lamps burned down the street in a chain of steady light. The tax man would come at midnight and find a street that glowed, and he would write it down in his book, and the book would not know what to do with a street that would not stay dark.

The girl stepped out of the shadows. She had a loaf of bread under her coat, stolen or saved, Corin did not ask. She looked at the last lamp, then at him.

"You lit the wrong lamp, boy," she said. "That one is mine."

She was gone before he could answer, slipping between the houses with the loaf and the chalk, leaving him alone in the glow of a street that had just decided not to die.

Corin walked home through his own light. The lamp room was dark when he reached it. Pell had gone to bed without a word, which was the old man's way of saying he had been afraid.

He lay down on his cot and felt the warmth on his arm, under the sleeve, where the lamp's flame had burned a page into his skin. It did not hurt. It felt like a word being written that he could almost read.

The book came to him in the dark. It was not a book he could touch, and not a dream. It was a ledger the size of a window, open in front of him, and its pages turned themselves to a fresh leaf.

A line wrote itself in ink the color of lamp oil:

"Debt recorded: the realm owes Corin Vale one light."

The ink dried, and the book waited, and the city's ash fell on the roof like the slow breathing of something that had been asleep for a hundred years.

The tax man arrived at midnight and found the street burning against the wind. He stood at the end of Flame Street with his book and his lantern, and he counted forty-two lights where the records promised him nothing.

He wrote nothing down. Some sums, the collectors had learned, could not be trusted until they were understood.

The book turned to a second page, and wrote a second line.

"Entry: the debt of the throne. Status: uncollected. Collector: none."

Corin read it in the dark, and the warmth on his arm spread to his chest, and somewhere in the city, in a quarter that had not seen a lamp lit in a hundred years, something answered the entry with a sound like a struck match.

The street slept. The ash fell. The page glowed under his sleeve.

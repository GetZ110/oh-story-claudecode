# Epic Fantasy Prose Card

## Prose directive
Epic fantasy builds worlds with weight: names, oaths, lineages, and maps that matter to the plot. The prose should be assured and slightly formal in narration, letting dialogue carry the warmth. Every chapter must keep the stakes legible — what the protagonist stands to lose is never abstract. The genre voice favors concrete nouns and earned sentences: a march, a siege, a throne room, a winter that kills. When the world expands (new land, new court, new magic), it expands through events, not paragraphs of description.

## Opening hooks
- Cut in at the fault line: an oath being broken, a border crossed, an heir returning in disgrace, a war banner raised.
- First scene establishes the power geometry — who holds what, who owes whom — through action, not exposition.
- The protagonist carries a concrete burden (a name, a debt, a keep) that the world keeps calling in.

## Conflict engine
Power geometry + oath and blood debts + a world that punishes the unprepared. Conflict runs on resources and allegiance: what the protagonist can raise, hold, and pay for.

## Payoff & emotion release
Release comes from oaths kept at cost, battles won through preparation, a court forced to acknowledge a claim, and the small human moment inside the grand event. Release on visible proofs: a standard planted, a debt ledger closed, an enemy's name struck from a scroll.

## Dialogue & voice
Speakers carry rank and region in their speech: courts formal, soldiers plain, old powers cryptic. The protagonist's voice must be recognizable across fifty chapters — a catchphrase, a rhythm, a habit of understatement.

## Chapter-end hooks
Use a banner sighted, an oath broken elsewhere, a claimant arriving, a supply line cut, a winter coming early, or a name spoken in a room the protagonist cannot enter. The hook widens the map or deepens the oath.

## Scene granularity
The texture lands on oath-rings, war councils, muster rolls, siege engines, keeps, bread prices, names in ledgers, and the objects that carry the plot (a standard, a letter, a key). The genre's realism comes from logistics and consequences, not just grandeur.

## Prose landing points
Open on the fault line or the oath; conflict lands on power geometry and resources; endings land on a visible cost paid in public — a standard raised, an oath answered, a door opened.

## Early/mid/late play
- Early: establish the power geometry and the protagonist's burden; the first oath answered by chapter 3-5.
- Mid: alternate campaign and court; every victory costs something the protagonist cannot replace.
- Late: converge the lines at the final field; the ending settles the oaths, not just the battle.

## Pacing density
One visible world event per chapter (a council, a march, a letter, a siege engine built). No chapter of pure travel or pure court; the two must trade.

## This-chapter tradeoffs
Pick one grand element and one small human element per chapter; make the small one carry the chapter's emotion.

## Drift bans
No exposition paragraphs that stop the scene; no names that exist only to be impressive; no battles won by luck instead of preparation; no stakes so abstract the reader can't name what the protagonist loses.

## Sample passages

> 1. The banner had not flown in nineteen years, and the moth-eaten cloth smelled of cedar and old blood. Alaric unfurled it in the yard of the burned keep, where the snow had drifted over the threshold his father had died defending. The men he'd brought were twelve — a smith, two shepherds, a deserter from the garrison, and eight boys who'd decided they were men. "This is the whole muster," the smith said. "This is the beginning," Alaric said, and he drove the standard into the frozen ground with his own hands, because a standard that went up by proxy came down by proxy.

> 2. The war council had been arguing for two hours about where to march, and the map was wearing thin at the river crossings. Elara waited until the noise settled, then put her palm flat on the southern pass. "Not the pass," she said. "The granary." The lords looked at her the way they looked at all her suggestions — as if she'd spoken in a foreign language. "The pass is where the army is," said Lord Veyne. "The granary is where the army eats," she said. "Burn it, and the pass starves in a fortnight without a single sword drawn." The silence that followed was the longest of the council.

> 3. The siege had lasted ninety days, and the defenders had eaten the horses, the rats, and finally the leather. On the ninety-first morning the gates opened — not to surrender, but to a single figure walking out with a white cloth tied to a spear. The besieging general rode forward, expecting terms. The figure handed him a letter, and the letter said: the city yields nothing, but the winter is coming, and we have counted your fires. You have wood for six weeks. We have walls. Sit or go. The general read it twice. On the ninety-second day the army marched away, and the city's name entered the songs not as a conquest but as a question.

- Paragraph length: 2-4 sentences base; set-pieces (sieges, marches, oaths) 4-6, kept rare
- Pacing: one visible world event per chapter; a campaign or court arc every 10-15 chapters

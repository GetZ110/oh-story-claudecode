# Cozy Mystery Prose Card

## Prose directive
Cozy mystery keeps the stakes human and the violence off-page: the body is found, the question is who and why, and the blood stays in the past tense. The prose should be warm, observant, and lightly funny — the sleuth notices the pie and the alibi with equal attention. Every chapter advances the puzzle with at least one fair clue, and the tone never turns grim even when the truth is. The genre voice lives in the town: names, shops, feuds, and the weather that decides everything.

## Opening hooks
- Cut in at the discovery: a body in the bookstore, a murder weapon in the cake display, a missing person whose car is still in the driveway.
- First scene establishes the sleuth's position in the town — what they see because everyone tells them things.
- The sleuth is an amateur with a professional eye (baker, librarian, florist, innkeeper); the craft is the alibi-breaker.

## Conflict engine
A closed circle of suspects + small-town secrets + the sleuth's own reputation at risk. Conflict runs on motive and opportunity: who benefits, who lied, who had the quiet hatred nobody counted.

## Payoff & emotion release
Release comes from the clue that re-frames a suspect, the amateur craft that cracks the professional alibi, and the public reveal where the killer's face finally doesn't match the town's memory of them. Release on quiet proofs: the wrong pie recipe, the mismatched receipt, the dog that never barked.

## Dialogue & voice
Dialogue is the engine: gossip carries clues, evasion carries guilt, and the sleuth's friends talk in comfortable shorthand. Every character has a recognizable voice — the mayor who never finishes a sentence, the baker's rival who finishes everyone else's. No one monologues the solution.

## Chapter-end hooks
Use a clue that points at the wrong person convincingly, a suspect whose story changes under one question, a second victim's echo, a locked alibi cracking, or the sleuth realizing the killer knew they were asking. The hook advances the puzzle, never the dread.

## Scene granularity
The texture lands on shop inventories, receipt timestamps, potluck seating charts, weather reports, church socials, town council minutes, and the small domestic details that break alibis (a stained cuff, a borrowed car, a pie that takes six hours to bake and forty minutes to serve). Realism comes from procedure and community.

## Prose landing points
Open on the discovery; conflict lands on the suspect circle and town secrets; endings land on a clue re-framing someone — with the next question already visible.

## Early/mid/late play
- Early: establish the town and the sleuth's place in it; the body arrives by chapter 1-2.
- Mid: alternate interviews and clue-finds; every suspect gets one chapter in the light and one doubt.
- Late: the false solution collapses in public; the true reveal re-frames the early chapters; the ending restores the town's peace with one warmth beat.

## Pacing density
One fair clue and one red herring per 2-3 chapters. No chapter without a town texture beat; the community is the second protagonist.

## This-chapter tradeoffs
One suspect cleared or deepened, one clue advanced, per chapter. Keep the sleuth's amateur stakes alive — the shop, the reputation, the dog.

## Drift bans
No on-page gore or graphic violence; no killer introduced in the final chapter; no amateur sleuth who is secretly a detective; no motive that appears from nowhere. The puzzle must be solvable in principle, and the town must stay a town.

## Sample passages

> 1. The body was discovered at 7 a.m. by the mail carrier, propped in the armchair of the bookshop's reading corner like a customer who'd nodded off with a volume of poetry open on his chest. The cause of death would later be given as a fall — the ladder was right there, the ceiling-high shelves made it plausible. But Clara had restocked those shelves for twelve years, and the ladder never stood where the police photos showed it. The ladder lived against the back wall. Someone had moved it to point at the wrong shelf. And the book on his chest was one he'd told her, twice, he'd never read.

> 2. The alibi was beautiful. The mayor's brother had been at the church social from seven to nine, and forty people had seen him — forty people who'd also seen him eat three servings of the tuna casserole, which was why he'd missed the dessert table, which was where the victim's wife had stood talking to the florist for the entire evening. The casserole detail was the problem. The casserole had been baked by the victim's wife, who used a recipe that called for dill, and the mayor's brother had an allergy to dill that made his face swell. He'd eaten three servings. Which meant either the casserole wasn't hers — or he wasn't where he said he was. Clara wrote this down under "questions," and underlined it twice.

> 3. The receipts were the thing. Everyone in town saved their receipts — the grocery, the hardware store, the gas station — and the victim's wife was no exception. The police had taken the shoebox, logged every slip, found nothing. Clara asked to see the box anyway, because she'd noticed the wife saved receipts in order, by date, except for one gap: the week of the murder, the gas station slips were missing. Not the grocery slips. Not the pharmacy. Only the gas station. "You don't drive," she said to the wife. "Your car's been in the shop for a month." The wife's smile stayed perfectly in place. "I don't," she agreed. "That's why it's strange, isn't it?" It was, Clara thought. It was strange enough to be a question. And she knew exactly who drove that car.

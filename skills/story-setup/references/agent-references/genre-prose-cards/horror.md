# Horror Prose Card

## Prose directive
Horror prose works by restriction: the camera stays nailed to the protagonist's senses, and what they cannot see is always scarier than what they can. The prose should be precise and ordinary in the calm passages — normal sentences, normal objects — so the first wrong detail lands like a crack in glass. Fear is written through the body: cold, hair, breath, the small muscle that won't relax. The genre voice favors short sentences at the peak and longer, patient ones during the approach; escalation, never a flat scream.

## Opening hooks
- Cut in at the small wrongness: a door ajar, a photo with one face changed, a sound with no source, a rule that must not be broken.
- First scene establishes the normal world hard — rent, work, a loved one — so the horror has something to take.
- The protagonist is practical and skeptical; their attempts to rationalize are part of the dread.

## Conflict engine
A rule or presence that escalates + information that comes too slowly + a protagonist whose every survival move is also a compromise. Conflict runs on the price of knowing: each answer costs another piece of the normal world.

## Payoff & emotion release
Release comes from the escape that costs something, the rule finally understood, and the thing that was following revealing what it wanted. Release on concrete proofs: a door that stays closed, a light that holds, a name learned too late.

## Dialogue & voice
Dialogue is sparse and functional; people under threat don't elaborate. Characters say the wrong thing at the wrong volume. No character explains the horror — the reader assembles it from reactions.

## Chapter-end hooks
Use a rule violated one sentence too late, a presence confirmed without being seen, a safe place turning wrong, a witness changing their story, or the quiet detail that re-frames the whole chapter. The hook must make the reader check the room.

## Scene granularity
The texture lands on phone batteries, locked doors, hallways, timestamps, the dark between streetlights, food going bad, and the small domestic objects that turn hostile (a mirror, a child's drawing, a thermostat). Realism comes from the mundane being exactly as fragile as it is.

## Prose landing points
Open on the small wrongness; conflict lands on the escalating rule and its price; endings land on a concrete cost paid or a rule understood — with the next violation already visible.

## Early/mid/late play
- Early: establish the normal world and the first wrong detail; skepticism buys the reader time to care.
- Mid: escalate the rule and restrict resources (power, signal, exits); every brief return of safety sharpens the next wave.
- Late: reveal what the presence wanted and what the survival cost; the ending keeps one question open — the door is closed, but the door was never the point.

## Pacing density
One wrong detail or rule escalation per chapter minimum. No chapter of pure dread with nothing happening; the horror must move, even if the movement is slow.

## This-chapter tradeoffs
Give one answer, take one piece of safety, per chapter. Keep the protagonist's practical voice intact even at the worst.

## Drift bans
No gore as the scare itself; no monsters explained into boredom; no characters who fail to react because the plot needs them to walk forward; no happy endings that un-pay the cost. Fear is made by restraint, not volume.

## Sample passages

> 1. The apartment had one flaw, and the landlord had mentioned it exactly once, in passing, the way people mention things they hope you'll forget: the door to the utility closet didn't lock from the outside, only from the inside. Mara laughed it off, used it for storage, never thought about it. On the third night she woke at 3 a.m. to the sound of the closet door clicking shut — from the inside. She lay very still and listened. After a long moment, something inside the closet began to breathe, slow and patient, like it had been waiting for her to wake up to begin.

> 2. The rule was simple, printed on the orientation sheet: after midnight, do not answer the house phone. It rang at 12:03. It rang again at 12:07. On the third ring, the caller ID lit up with her own name. She let it ring, counting, the way the sheet advised. At 12:11 it stopped, and the silence that followed was heavier than the ringing had been. Then the phone rang a fourth time — and it wasn't the house phone. It was the one in her pocket. She looked at the screen. The caller was her mother. She answered, because it was her mother, and a voice that was almost her mother's said: "Why didn't you pick up the house phone?"

> 3. They found the drawing taped to the refrigerator on the fourth morning. A house, a sun, two stick figures holding hands — the kind of drawing a child makes. His daughter was five. She had not drawn it, she said, with her face very still and her hand tight around her juice cup. The figures in the drawing had no faces. "Who's that supposed to be?" he asked. "That's you," she said, pointing at the taller one. "And that's me." She pointed at the smaller one. Then she pointed at the third figure — the one he hadn't noticed, standing in the yard outside the house, taller than the roof. "That's the man who watches us when you're at work." He looked at the drawing for a long time. The third figure was holding something that might have been a key.

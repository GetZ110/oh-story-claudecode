# Dark Romance Prose Card

## Prose directive
Dark romance keeps the power imbalance raw and the heroine's consent always in play. The atmosphere is pressurized: closed rooms, watched movements, debts that cannot be paid. The romantic tension must never read as simple rescue — the hero is dangerous first and soft last, and every kindness from him carries a cost the heroine can name. Keep the prose tight, sensory, and slightly colder than the genre's emotional temperature; the heat comes from what is withheld.

## Opening hooks
- Cut in at the moment the heroine realizes she cannot leave: a signed debt, a family traded for protection, a room with a locked door.
- First scene establishes the leash — what he holds over her — before any attraction.
- The heroine is never passive: she negotiates, lies, plans, and resists inside the cage.

## Conflict engine
Desire + danger + a power gap that never fully closes. The conflict runs on what she would lose by staying and what she would lose by leaving; both must be real.

## Payoff & emotion release
Payoffs come from the heroine bending the rules back, the dangerous man showing one unguarded moment, rivals miscalculating, and protection turning public. Release on actions: a hand removed from her throat, a debt ledger burned, a door left unlocked.

## Dialogue & voice
The lead speaks short, flat, and absolute — commands, not requests — until a single line slips past his control. The heroine's voice is defiance in smaller and smaller packages: she learns which words cost her. Subtext carries the intimacy; nothing is confessed twice.

## Chapter-end hooks
Use a debt coming due, a watcher revealed, the past he buried surfacing, a boundary crossed in public, or the heroine's quiet counter-move landing. The hook always raises the price of the relationship.

## Scene granularity
Pressure lands on ledgers, contracts, security footage, locked doors, safe houses, scars, and the things he does with his hands when he thinks she isn't watching. The dark-world texture comes from consequences, not shock value.

## Prose landing points
Open on the debt or the cage; conflict lands on control and its loopholes; endings land on a visible cost paid, a line crossed, or the leash changing hands.

## Early/mid/late play
- Early: establish the leash and her resistance inside it; attraction arrives as a liability.
- Mid: alternate his control with her counters; every trust given is a risk taken.
- Late: settle the external threat and make the choice of staying a free one — or a chosen cage.

## Pacing density
One power-exchange scene per chapter minimum. No chapters of pure brooding; the pressure must move.

## This-chapter tradeoffs
Name one cost and one loophole per chapter. Keep the heroine's agency visible even at her lowest point.

## Drift bans
No romanticizing abuse as love; no heroine whose will simply evaporates; no violence used as spice; no redemption that erases the ledger. Consent and consequence stay explicit.

## Sample passages

> 1. He didn't raise his voice. That was the worst part. "You left the building." "I went to the pharmacy." "You didn't ask." She held up the receipt. "Tylenol. Your head." He looked at the receipt for a long moment, then took it, folded it once, and put it in his pocket. He didn't say thank you. But the door to her room was unlocked that night, and it hadn't been unlocked in four months.

> 2. The debt was her father's. The collateral was her. The contract said "employment," which meant she cooked his meals, kept his house, and did not leave the grounds. On the seventh day she found the flaw: the gate code was the same as his mother's birthday, and his mother had been dead for nine years. She filed it away like a coin in a pocket. Not for escape. For leverage.

> 3. "You're afraid of me." She said it flat, like a fact she was filing. He didn't deny it. "Good." "That's the problem," she said. "I used to be." He went very still. "What changed?" "You could have taken anything from me tonight. You took the broken glass out of my hand first." He said nothing. His jaw worked once, and then he walked out, and she understood she'd just won something she wasn't sure she wanted.

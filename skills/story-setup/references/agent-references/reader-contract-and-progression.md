# Reader Contract & Endgame Reserve

This file is `story-long-write`'s authoritative reference on reader contract, protagonist agency (narrative agency/dominance), interest safety, and stage progression. The insights are distilled from authorized community samples; most samples are problem/avoidance-pit posts, so they are non-statistical consensus and cannot replace genre-, platform-, or benchmark-specific judgment.

**Safety boundary**: this file must not contain source-site private data, access credentials, cookies, tokens, request signatures, private identifiers, or anything that could reverse-engineer private accounts/request environments.

## 1. Core concepts

### Reader contract
- Reader contract = the reading pleasure promised jointly by the opening, genre, protagonist configuration, and narrative voice.
- Before writing, confirm what the reader is here for: the protagonist solving problems, witnessing an ensemble, watching a mentor lift successors, or institutional cooperation-building? The same plot carries different risk under different contracts.
- Genre boundary precedes individual taboos: the moment high-level institutions appear (government, sect headquarters, transnational organizations, multinational companies), an institutional/faction storyline may open; first check whether this book promised that kind of play from the opening.

### Protagonist agency: causal rights + settlement rights
- Protagonist agency is primarily **causal rights + settlement rights**, not screen time; it never requires the protagonist to do everything personally or monopolize every shot.
- Irreplaceability first shows as causal rights: through decisions, setups, delegations, irreplaceable information/resources, or actively chosen key consequences, the protagonist determines why things happen, how they enter, and how they turn.
- Settlement rights = core benefits, recognition, power, or key consequences return to the deserved party per the existing promise. Leveraging others, teaming up, being saved, accepting guidance, or having others execute local actions are all fine, as long as causation and settlement are not silently stolen.
- Career/power-fantasy genres are especially sensitive: readers usually expect the protagonist to control resources, make decisions, bear or choose consequences, and take back visible benefits.
- Genre exception (direct-payoff fiction): Webnovel-style "war god / son-in-law / comeuppance / urban supernatural" books — the protagonist acting personally and crushing opponents face to face is itself the contract delivery; agency cannot be used to let the protagonist coast while subordinates take the glory. Only setup/scheming/management/ensemble/mentor genres default to behind-the-scenes orchestration and delivery through others.

### Expectation ownership
- Promised delivery/highlights belong to the protagonist or other promise owners already committed in this volume or unit; side characters may execute local actions but cannot silently steal expectation ownership.
- Ensemble, mentor, or institutional-cooperation books may explicitly give expectation ownership to team members/students/institutional characters; the key is to promise in advance and settle per the contract, not to justify scene-stealing after the fact.

### Key-node four questions
Ask uniformly at key nodes during outlining, prose, and review; answers may point at the protagonist or at other owners already promised this volume — the protagonist need not personally perform every action:
1. Who decides why things happen / how they enter?
2. Who makes the irreplaceable key choice?
3. Who bears or chooses the key consequence?
4. To whom are core benefits/recognition/power finally settled?

### Interest safety & delivery attribution
- Interest safety = readers trust that the core selling point they follow will not be confiscated, diluted, gifted away, or handed to unrelated characters to digest.
- Delivery attribution = whoever sets up, bears the pressure, and chooses should receive the main emotional return; bystander/side-character reactions may amplify a payoff but cannot replace the protagonist's payoff.
- Core assets (cheat, key resources, identity trump cards, exclusive technology, key evidence) must not be handed over, exposed to outside institutions, or taken over by superiors without exchange. If they must be surrendered, a visible exchange is required: power, protection, resources, channels, recognition, claims, information, stage victories, or initiative in a larger situation.

### Promise debt
- Every planted promise (the protagonist will counterattack, resources will upgrade, identity will be revealed, romance will advance) creates a promise debt.
- Promise debt may be deferred, but it pays interest: stage progress, new information, situation changes, emotional release, or a clearer next goal.
- Delaying delivery with explanations, setting, or institutional process adds rationalization debt.

### Endgame reserve & progression pace

The root cause of "nothing left to write" is two kinds of **macro resource** being burned early — it has little to do with how much a single chapter grows. Manage these two, open single-chapter density, and you neither overdraw nor suffocate.

**The two kinds of exhaustible macro resource (set at book opening, unlocked volume by volume)**
- **Endgame trump cards**: the head rival, ultimate truth/identity, cheat ceiling, identity/status endpoint, and the final confirmation of core emotion. One-time resources; once used, gone. At book opening, list them in the "Endgame trump cards & power-up ladder" section of `setting/genre-positioning.md`, each marked with the earliest volume/stage it may unlock (back-derive from the endgame into volume-level milestones; one big milestone per volume).
- **Power-up ladder**: remaining rungs of realm/level/map/faction tiers. At book opening, run a ladder check — system rungs × words per rung ≥ whole-book target words; too few rungs is a design disease fixed at design time (lengthen the system / add map layers), not by rationing chapters. Enemies/goals/maps unlock as a **tiered ladder**; no skipping to instantly kill the top boss / solve the ultimate goal.

**Open density per chapter / per story unit (the payoff source — do not clamp)**
- Each story unit sets 1 **primary push line** (one of the progression lines: power/resource/identity/relationship/info/map/institution/faction/career/emotional-certainty — carrying this round's biggest climax); other lines get **result gains** naturally as the plot develops (light touch to big jump all fine). **One battle many gains — one big win that simultaneously breaks through, confiscates, recruits factions, raises prestige, and wins the girl — is allowed, common, and good design; give it.**
- Chapter-level advancement tiers by platform/genre: fast-paced (Webnovel/payoff fiction) keeps the floor of "each chapter needs a visible event or payoff advance"; only slow-burn/homestead/emotional books relax to "changing one of the seven state types: goal / risk / information / relationship / resource / identity / emotional stance". Low-pressure/transition chapters are carried by small visible changes; no forced per-chapter climax.
- After a climax/delivery, a short low-pressure passage may follow, letting the reader first take small visible gains/rewards before the next pressure round; no mandatory immediate crisis after delivery.
- Optional tool: action cost / success cost. Important successes may carry time, relationship, exposure, resource-consumption, or new-hostility costs; low-pressure/transition/pure-reward chapters do not force costs.

**Overspeed determination (the overdraw two questions)**
1. Did you use an endgame trump card this stage should not unlock yet (rival finished, ultimate truth, identity endpoint, cheat ceiling)?
2. Did any progression line near its ceiling with no ladder rung left?

Answer "yes" to either = genuine overdraw warning → roll back or revise the outline; both "no" = multi-line rise passes. This judgment is an experience rule; tiers and milestones adjust to this book's genre, platform, and benchmark — not a hard gate.

### Rationalization debt
- Explanation cost is used to justify why institutional response/takeover, confidentiality, resource mobilization, cross-region enforcement, and organizational collaboration hold.
- Check: do these explanations serve the core emotion? If the explanation itself swallows payoff, immersion, or protagonist highlights, shorten it, convert it to a visible exchange, or demote the institutional response to bystander/obstacle/resource interface.

## 2. Institutions & bystander design

- Mentioning government, sect headquarters, transnational organizations, or multinational companies is not inherently toxic; they are just high-level narrative switches.
- National cooperation / government-collaboration stories can count as contract safe, provided the opening promised institutional cooperation, the protagonist stays irreplaceable, and gains visible power, protection, resources, benefits, or higher action authority.
- If the book promises individual turnaround, career control, or an exclusive cheat, outside institutions taking over core problems usually requires a strong exchange plus the protagonist's active choice.
- Bystander/reaction layers suit: shock, misjudgment, public-opinion spread, resource offers, taking sides, witnessing the protagonist's choice. Avoid letting bystanders take revenge for the protagonist, make the final judgment for the protagonist, or take core benefits for the protagonist.

## 3. Risk levels

| Level | Definition | Handling |
|---|---|---|
| Contract safe | consistent with the opening genre contract; the protagonist still holds key choices/irreplaceable contributions; benefit attribution clear | writable; make exchanges and benefits visible |
| Needs reinforcement | may dilute protagonist highlights or advance too fast, but fixable via setup, exchange, cost, or genre declaration | before writing, add reasons, costs, or restage as stage-level rather than endgame delivery |
| Contract broken | core selling point confiscated; voluntary cession causing **long-term net benefit negative + protagonist control/causal rights declining** (especially without a visible strategic exchange); unrelated characters stealing expectation ownership; core assets surrendered or taken over without exchange | fix the outline; strategic concessions may trade local spotlight/resources for greater control, future benefits, relationship/institutional power, information advantage, or the delivery of already-promised ensemble/mentor highlights — do not flash red on the cession itself |

## 4. Good/bad examples and exceptions

### Protagonist voluntarily ceding the spotlight in career/power fantasy
- Red-light combination: cession simultaneously causes long-term net benefit negative, protagonist control/causal rights declining, and no visible strategic exchange; not every voluntary cession counts as contract broken.
- Good: the protagonist delegates execution to subordinates while keeping the plan, key resources, final negotiation, and risk-bearing; or trades local spotlight/resources for greater control, future benefits, relationship/institutional power, information advantage. Subordinate performance in turn proves the protagonist's judgment/management.
- Exception: if the book promises "mentor / management organization / ensemble growth" from the opening, the protagonist's highlights may be the training system, talent-spotting, and key backstopping.

### Giving away resources or core assets
- Bad: the protagonist hands exclusive technology, cheat products, or key evidence to an institution unconditionally, gets verbal thanks, and the institution then drives the main line.
- Good: the protagonist exchanges for licenses, protection, channels, voice, exclusive benefits, or a bigger stage, and keeps irreplaceable control points.

### Unrelated characters avenging the protagonist
- Bad: an enemy that oppressed the protagonist for many chapters is dealt with by a passing strongman or random accident; the protagonist made no choice, took no action, and gained no cognition/resources/status change.
- Good: side characters may assist or witness, but key evidence, the setup, the decision, the public verdict, or the final counterattack are completed by the protagonist.
- Exception: tragedy, mystery, and ensemble-war stories may let others complete local actions, but the protagonist must gain a new core task, cost, or cognitive advance.

### Burning the endgame reserve early (multi-line rise itself is fine)
- Bad: one story unit burns the book-level trump cards at once — solving the head rival, revealing the ultimate identity, confirming the endgame identity, or hitting the cheat ceiling directly — leaving no big goal to write later.
- Good: this unit lets the protagonist win one battle with many gains (breakthrough + loot + prestige all rising), but all inside the system rungs, never touching endgame trump cards; the rival only drops one tier, the truth only reveals one layer; big trump cards stay for later volumes to repay promise debt.

### Legitimate ensemble / mentor story exceptions
- Ensemble contract: every POV has its own goal and repayment; the protagonist is not the only payoff source; the volume contract should state who holds the highlights this volume.
- Mentor contract: the mentor-protagonist's highlights are designing training, seeing through traps, key backstopping, and bearing higher-level costs — not harvesting personally every time.
- Institutional-cooperation contract: the opening promises national/institutional collaboration; the protagonist gets clear power and benefits as expert, commander, key-resource holder, or irreplaceable executor.

## 5. Continuation & new-element debt

- **New-element debt**: introducing new maps, institutions, abilities, enemies, or mysteries both pays necessary setup costs and adds new promise debt; novelty cannot dodge old promises.
- **Genre-specific high risk**: in fulfillment-genre/ability-fantasy books, the protagonist repeatedly creating disasters through stupidity or avoidable incompetence, then having others clean up, continuously damages causal rights, control, and settlement trust; this is a high risk judged by genre contract, not an absolute ban for all stories.

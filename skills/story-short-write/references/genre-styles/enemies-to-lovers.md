# Genre Style Pack: Enemies-to-Lovers

> English short-form romance for Wattpad / Tapas / Inkitt / Radish / Dreame. Read `short-craft.md` first; this pack layers the genre-specific voice on top.
> One-line positioning: **two people who genuinely dislike (or ruin) each other are forced together until friction turns into want.** The reader wants the crackle of verbal warfare that slowly stops being a war, the moment the antagonism flips, and the surrender that still keeps both of them sharp.

---

## Narrative voice

First person (either lead, usually the female lead on Wattpad YA / Radish; either POV works on Inkitt) or close third — decide per platform: Wattpad YA romance is overwhelmingly first person; Inkitt/Radish vary by subgenre. The voice runs on **banter and internal contradiction**: the surface text denies what the body keeps doing ("I hated the way he laughed. I'd been watching him laugh for five years."). Internal monologue is snark-first, vulnerable second; the vulnerability slips out in the last clause.

Dual track: the **combat register** (sharp, score-keeping, competitive) and the **crack register** (the moment the armor slips — a softer line, a shared laugh, a stillness). The switch between them is the story's engine. POV is single-track with the POV lead's blind spots intact; if the other POV appears, it's a short planted chapter (Inkitt multi-POV is normal; Wattpad serials keep one POV with occasional bonus chapters).

## Opening patterns

A 120-220 word blurb opens with the forced proximity premise + one insulting line of dialogue; the body's first section re-shoots that scene with full dialogue texture.

| Opening type | Example |
|--------------|---------|
| The ruin they already caused | "I hated Callum Hart before I ever met him. He'd gotten my sister fired, my cousin evicted, and my mother's bakery health-coded, and he'd done it all from three states away." |
| Forced proximity announcement | "The email said the merger was final. The office directory said my new desk was eighteen inches from the man I'd reported to HR three times." |
| Wrong-place hatred | "The stranger who saved my seat on the train turned out to be the lawyer suing my father. I told him to get lost. He told me the seat was mine, the war wasn't." |
| The bet / the dare | "He bet me a hundred dollars I couldn't last one season working at his family's orchard. I bet him I could hate him the whole time. I was wrong about one of those things." |
| The wedding-ruiner | "I showed up to my ex's wedding to steal back the dog. The best man confiscated the leash, looked me up and down, and said, 'You must be the one he still talks about.'" |

## Hook motifs and density

The hook isn't injury escalation — it's **tension escalation with a clock**: every section adds one point of forced contact or one crack in the armor, and each section ends with a question mark over the relationship.

- **Forced proximity** (head motif): shared office, one apartment, one car, one reality-show team, one hospital room, co-parenting a dog. The setup locks them in the same frame; the frame does the romance.
- **Scorekeeping**: they keep count of wins/losses, favors, and insults; the ledger becomes the love language ("That's three times I've cooked for you. You owe me a pie.").
- **The misunderstanding with teeth**: the hate has a real origin (a ruined deal, a stolen credit, a family war) — and the truth about it arrives late, re-routing all the prior friction into hurt/comfort.
- **Public vs private switch**: combative in front of everyone, soft in the one private scene — the private scene is the reader's reward and the midpoint fuel.
- **The almost-moment**: interrupted kisses, near-confessions, shared silence at 2 a.m. — the almost-moment is the chapter-end hook; it gets interrupted at least twice before it lands.
- **Witnesses who see it first**: the best friend / grandmother / coworker who notices the tension before the leads do; their one-liners are the reader's permission to root.
- **The grovel bridge**: when the hate finally admits defeat, the hateful one must apologize in action, not speech — the fix-it move (shows up, fixes the thing they broke) is the payoff the reader waits for.

## Emotional intensity and patterns (intensity 7)

The engine is **push-pull with a rising floor**: the lows are low (real anger, real hurt from the origin conflict), the highs get higher as the armor thins. Emotion is shown through behavior — the POV lead denies, deflects, then acts.

- Denial beats: "I wasn't watching him. I was watching the door. The door was interesting." Keep them short and funny; the reader must be able to see through every one.
- The crack: one physical tell that betrays the lead (the hand that lingers, the laugh that comes out honest, the glance that isn't aimed at the door).
- The fight scene is also a love scene: the same intensity, different target. The argument where they finally tell the truth about the origin conflict is the emotional climax of the first half.
- The low point: the misunderstanding resurfaces at the worst moment (public reveal, third party, timing), and one of them says the unforgivable thing — this is the section where the reader's heart is actually in their mouth.

Sample passage (register demonstration):

> He held the elevator for me. That was new. It was 6:40 a.m., he had a coffee in each hand, and he was holding the door with his elbow like it cost him nothing.
> "Don't look so suspicious," he said. "The coffee's for me and the other one's also for me."
> I stepped in. "Then why do you look like I caught you being nice?"
> "Habit. I hear it's contagious."
> We rode twenty floors in silence. When the doors opened he said, "The one with the extra sugar is on the front desk. Somebody left it there. It's been sitting for five minutes, which is a shame."
> I didn't see the smile on his face. I was busy not looking at him.

Second sample (the low point — the origin conflict surfaces):

> "You think I don't know why you're really here?" I said. My voice came out level, which surprised me. "The merger. The orchard. The seat next to mine. It's all the same play you ran on my father."
> He went very still. That was answer enough.
> "Your father's company was already dead when I touched it," he said, finally. "The man who killed it is sitting on your family's board. I've been trying to get close enough to prove it for four years." He set something on the table between us — a folder, worn at the edges. "This was supposed to be the end of the play. It's your family's file. I was going to bring it to you the day you told me to leave."
> I didn't open it. I looked at him instead, at the man I'd spent a year hating on a deadline.
> "You had four years," I said.
> "I had four years," he agreed. "I spent three of them being the villain you needed me to be."

## Dialogue register and killer lines

Dialogue ratio 50-65%. The power marker is who breaks the rhythm first: the banter is a duel where losing your cool loses the point, so each character fights with short, dry, complete lines.

**① The combat register (insults that are actually attention)**
- "I've met people I dislike more. Give me a minute, I'll find one."
- "You're not wrong. You're just loud about being right."
- "That's the third time you've watched me walk away. I'm starting to think you like the view."
- "I don't hate you," he said. "I hate that I keep noticing you."

**② The crack register (armor slips, mid-fight)**
- "I don't want to win this one," he said, quietly. "I just want you to stop being right about me."
- "You ruined my family's business." / "I know. I was trying to save mine. I'm sorry it took me this long to say that out loud."
- "If I apologize, will you stop being scared of me?" / "I'm not scared of you." / "You are. You flinch when I get close. I've noticed."

**③ The witness register (friends/family commenting)**
- "You two fight like people who've already decided not to leave."
- "He fixed your car, your roof, and your mother's kitchen sink. At some point you have to admit he's not the villain of this story."

**④ The surrender register (the payoff, short and specific)**
- "I'm not saying I was wrong. I'm saying I'd rather be wrong with you than right alone."
- "You can keep the ledger. I was never keeping score of the same thing."

## Move library

| Move | How to use | Example |
|------|-----------|---------|
| Forced proximity | Lock them in one frame; let the frame do the romance | One office, one house, one car, one dog |
| Scorekeeping | The running tally becomes intimacy | "You owe me a pie." "You owe me three apologies." |
| Misunderstanding with teeth | Real origin conflict, true story arrives late | The business ruin was his father's, not his; the lie was survival |
| Almost-moment | Tease the kiss/confession twice, interrupt it, then land it | Phone rings, door opens, someone walks in |
| Public-private switch | Combat in public, soft in private; the private scene resets the tension | A harsh review in front of the team, then the car ride home |
| Witness commentary | A third party names the tension first | "You two fight like people who've decided not to leave." |
| Fix-it grovel | The apology is action, not speech | He rebuilds the thing he broke, brings the coffee, shows up |
| The truth scene | One scene re-routes all prior hate into hurt/comfort | The argument where the origin conflict finally gets told straight |
| Idiot-plot avoidance | Misunderstandings must break by the next section, not drag three chapters | The eavesdropped line is replayed and explained before the end of the section after |
| The last-chapter surrender | The love confession lands in action + one short line, not a speech | He's already at her door with the thing she needs; the line is "I was wrong to let you think I didn't care." |

## Rhythm skeleton

```
Blurb (forced proximity + one insulting line)
→ Section 1: re-shoot the blurb scene, establish the war
→ Forced proximity locks in (shared frame)
→ Banter cycles (one crack per section, one almost-moment per 2-3 sections)
→ The misunderstanding's true story surfaces (truth scene, mid-point)
→ Hurt/comfort phase (friction re-routed; the low point near 70%)
→ Fix-it grovel + public acknowledgment
→ Surrender + happy ending (short, specific, no epilogue speechifying)
```

Dense where the crack happens (slow-motion the almost-moments), sparse in transitions. Never let a misunderstanding outlive one section without movement toward resolution — English readers abandon serials over dragged idiot plots.

---

## Ending patterns

- **The surrender**: the last fight ends with the shortest true line; the kiss/commitment lands immediately after, one concrete action (the key, the ring, the move-in box) instead of a speech.
- **The callback**: the opening insult is returned, re-read, as an inside joke.
- **The public flip**: the couple that everyone watched fight becomes the couple everyone watches stay — one scene, witnesses' reactions, done.
- **The bonus POV chapter** (Wattpad/Tapas serial style): his perspective on the moment he fell; short, sweet, no new conflict.
- Epilogue optional; if present, keep it one scene of settled domesticity with one last bit of banter. HE is a hard constraint for the romance packs: no open ending, no death, no resurrected ex.

## Genre-specific tuning (deviations from the general de-AI rules)

These read as AI-flavor elsewhere but are selling points here — **don't cut them**:

- **Snarky denial internal monologue** ("I wasn't watching him. I was watching the door."): keep; it's the genre's main engine for both comedy and tension.
- **Banter with complete short lines**: keep the duel rhythm; don't flatten it into polite exchange.
- **Almost-moment repetition**: the interrupted kiss is deliberate; don't treat it as a loop and "fix" it early.
- **Witness one-liners**: keep; they're the reader's permission slip.
- **The fix-it grovel**: keep apology-by-action; don't replace it with a long verbal apology speech.
- **Name-calling between leads in combat scenes**: fine when it's banter; the cruelty must never be one-sided real abuse — if a lead is genuinely demeaning (name-calling about worth, not wit), that's dark-romance territory, not this pack.

Still avoid the genuine AI-flavor list: "the kind of X that Y", "couldn't help but", "a wave of X washed over", "in that moment", "eyes widened", "heart raced", essay-voice connectives ("furthermore", "needless to say"), and emotion-by-summary instead of behavior.

> Punctuation: body prose follows `short-format.md` — no `...` runs, no `--`, em dashes at most one per line; pauses go through short sentences, action beats, and line breaks. Ellipses in quoted platform-verbatim text are the only exception.

## Structural formulas (by length)

- **Workplace/family-war (8-11 sections)**: 1-2 establish the war + the origin conflict → 3-4 forced proximity, banter + almost-moments → 5 truth scene (origin conflict re-read) → 6-7 the low point (misunderstanding resurfaces) → 8 fix-it grovel → 9 surrender + HE. First person; one POV; the antagonist is the situation plus one third-party spoiler, never the love interest.
- **The bet/dare premise (8-10 sections)**: premise set in the blurb → the bet's rules as the chapter structure (each section = one rule being bent) → the rule-break confession → surrender. Comedy-forward; the bet itself is the miscommunication that must be openly voided at the end.
- **Second-chance-adjacent (8-11 sections)**: see second-chance.md for the estrangement mechanics; the difference here is the pair never dated — the war is unresolved attraction with a history. The history scene (how the hatred started) is a planted flashback around 60%.

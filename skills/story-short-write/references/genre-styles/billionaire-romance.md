# Genre Style Pack: Billionaire Romance

> English short-form romance for Dreame / GoodNovel / Galatea / Radish / Inkitt / Wattpad. Read `short-craft.md` first; this pack layers the genre-specific voice on top.
> One-line positioning: **an ordinary-life heroine and a power-gap hero (CEO, heir, magnate) collide through contract, rescue, or revenge, and the fantasy is being seen as a person by someone who sees everyone as an asset.** The reader wants the Cinderella elevation, the money as both threat and safety, and the moment the power gap inverts and he is the one who has to earn.

---

## Narrative voice

First person (the heroine) for Dreame/GoodNovel/Radish (market default); close third works on Wattpad/Tapas anthology pieces. The voice runs on **class-anchored observation**: prices, brands, and power signals are narrated with the heroine's eye for what they mean about people ("the watch cost more than my mother's surgery; he checked it while I talked"). Internal monologue alternates between the leveling register (she refuses to be awed) and the private register (she is, a little, and hates it).

Dual track: the **working register** (her competence: job, side-hustle, family responsibilities — the money fantasy needs a heroine with a life) and the **fish-out-of-water register** (the gala, the private jet, the boardroom — narrated with humor and dignity, never cringe). His POV appears only in short planted chapters (Wattpad/Inkitt bonus chapters; Dreame/GoodNovel keep one POV).

## Opening patterns

A 120-220 word blurb opens with the collision + the money/dignity conflict named; the body's first section re-shoots the collision.

| Opening type | Example |
|--------------|---------|
| The mistaken identity | "The hotel thought I was the event planner and handed me his itinerary. He thought I was the new assistant and handed me his coffee. By the time anyone corrected anything, I'd already told the CEO of Hartwell Group his tie was crooked." |
| The contract offer | "He didn't offer me a job. He offered to buy the debt my father left me — at a discount, with a smile, 'as a favor to the family that raised me well.' I told him the price, doubled, or I'd take the debt to his competitors." |
| The rescue with a bill | "He paid my mother's hospital bill and pinned the receipt to my door with a note: 'You'll work it off. I have standards.' He didn't know I'd already been his accountant's auditor for three years." |
| The revenge premise | "He ruined my family's company when I was sixteen. Ten years later I interviewed for his personal-assistant job with his own merger documents in my bag. He hired me. He said I reminded him of someone." |
| The gala spill | "I spilled champagne on the most expensive man in the room. He looked at the stain, then at my dress, and said, 'That's a forty-thousand-dollar suit. Yours is borrowed. I'll be billing you.' I told him he could bill my attorney." |

## Hook motifs and density

The hook is **the power gap being tested and inverted**: every section either shows his world's cost (what his money can't buy, what it breaks) or her world's value (the competence he's never had, the life he's never lived). End sections on a threat to one of the two worlds.

- **The power-gap collision** (head motif): the difference in money/status is the engine, not the backdrop — the heroine's worth is measured against his money and found larger, repeatedly, in concrete scenes (the negotiation she wins, the life he can't buy).
- **The contract / arrangement**: employment, debt-work, fake-engagement, marriage-of-convenience — the arrangement is the frame; its rules bending is the romance.
- **The competence reveal**: the heroine is quietly excellent (auditor, nurse, engineer, baker, lawyer-to-be); the reveal scene where he watches her work is the midpoint attraction beat.
- **The money as weapon**: money is used against her (the rival, the family, the clause) and later by her (the leverage, the exit, the contract she signs herself). Never let the heroine's victory come from passive rescue — she wins with information, skill, or terms.
- **The family price**: both families carry cost — his mother's scheming, her father's debts, the siblings he supports; family scenes are where the gap becomes human.
- **The first-class lesson**: a scene where his world teaches her a rule she refuses (the charity dinner, the non-disclosure, the "don't embarrass the name") — her refusal is the reader's cheer point.
- **The inversion beat**: the moment the power flips — she has the leverage (the evidence, the contract clause, the public stage) and he has to ask. The inversion is this genre's version of the payoff; it must be public, concrete, and earned by her action.
- **The grovel**: after the inversion, his apology is action (the fired rival, the paid debt, the unfrozen clause), not speeches.

## Emotional intensity and patterns (intensity 7)

The engine is **elevation with dignity**: the fantasy is being lifted into his world without losing herself. Emotion is named and landed on class-specific objects (the borrowed dress, the receipt, the clause number).

- The leveling register: dry, funny, unimpressed — the heroine narrates his world's absurdity with warmth, not envy.
- The private register: the moments she admits the dream (the hospital bill paid, the sister's tuition covered) — short, honest, immediately deflected by humor.
- The attraction register: his competence and restraint (the way he works, the way he waits) rather than his wallet — the reader falls for the man, not the money; the money stays as menace and safety.
- The cost register: the scenes where his money hurts people (the layoff he orders, the rival he buries, the mother he funds) — the heroine's discomfort is the moral compass; she must never shrug at his cruelty.
- The low point: the arrangement's worst clause activates (public exposure, the family's counter-offer, the falsified story) and she must choose between the deal and herself — this is the section where the reader's "take the money AND leave" voice and "stay" voice fight.
- The inversion: her leverage scene — public, concrete, she wins on her own terms; the grovel follows in action.

Sample passage (register demonstration):

> The gala was held in a room that cost more to light than my mother's ward cost to run. I kept that thought in my pocket like a stone.
> He found me at the dessert table, which was honest of us both.
> "You're the only person here who's eaten anything," he said.
> "It's the best part of the evening." I picked up a second tart. "Your caterer is excellent. You should pay them more."
> "You've been here an hour and that's your review of my company."
> "You asked for honesty. I assumed the clause was still in the contract."
> He laughed — a real one, not the boardroom one. The sound surprised him more than it surprised me. "I'm not used to being corrected by my own hires."
> "Then you're not used to hiring well," I said, and took a bite. "Happy to be your first."
> He looked at me the way he looked at spreadsheets: carefully, and for much longer than the data required.

Second sample (the inversion — her leverage):

> The boardroom went quiet when she walked in. That was the first time anything in that room had gone quiet all year.
> She set one folder on the table and didn't sit down. "The acquisition terms you voted on last night had a clause buried on page forty-one: the family holds a right of first refusal on the new entity's board seats." She tapped the folder. "The family exercised that right at eight this morning. This is the registration."
> "The family?" the CFO said. "Your father's estate was settled. There is no family."
> "There's me." She opened the folder and let the pages fan across the table like a hand of cards. "I own forty-two percent of the holding company, I own the clause, and I own the date. You have two choices: seat me on the board, or explain to the press why the acquisition closed without the majority owner's signature."
> The room looked at the man at the head of the table. He'd gone very still, which was his version of applause.
> "You've been auditing me for three years," he said. "I thought I knew every number you owned."
> "You knew the numbers I showed you," she said. "You never asked about the ones I kept."

## Dialogue register and killer lines

Dialogue ratio 45-60%. The power marker is contract language: he speaks in terms and clauses; she answers in plain sentences that name what the terms actually mean. Her plainness is the power move.

**① The contract register (his terms, her counters)**
- "You'll sign the NDA, the conduct agreement, and the calendar." / "I'll sign the NDA. The conduct agreement gets a clause: no conduct I haven't agreed to. And my calendar stays mine."
- "This is a business arrangement. I don't do feelings." / "Good. I don't do men who lie about business."
- "I own everything in this room." / "You own everything in this room. I own the number that keeps it standing. Remember that when you get bored of me."

**② The leveling register (her world answers his)**
- "That watch costs more than my sister's tuition." / "It does. My sister's in prison." — (variant: keep the counters sharp, never cruel for sport)
- "I'm not impressed by your money. I'm impressed that you learned my coffee order. That's the only thing here you didn't pay someone to know."
- "The private jet was nice. The pilot told me you're scared of heights. I find that adorable, and I will be using it."

**③ The cost register (she names the collateral)**
- "You fired three hundred people and you're annoyed the headline made you look bad." / "I'm annoyed the headline was wrong. I'd have said 'four hundred' and owned it."
- "You're not a villain. You're a man with a lot of leverage and no one telling him no. That's worse, because villains at least know they're villains."

**④ The surrender register (the inversion's payoff)**
- "I'm not asking you to sign anything. I'm asking you to stay because I'm asking."
- "The company's yours. The contract's void. I kept the only clause that mattered: you."

## Rhythm skeleton

```
Blurb (collision + money/dignity conflict)
→ Section 1: the collision re-shot, the arrangement offered
→ His world tours (cost register + first-class lessons) alternating with her world (competence + family)
→ The competence reveal; the arrangement's rules start bending
→ The family price + the first crisis of the arrangement
→ The low point: the worst clause activates; her choice
→ The inversion beat (public, on her terms)
→ The action grovel + surrender
→ HE: settled, her world intact inside his
```

Dense in the competence reveal, the inversion, and the grovel; sparse in the galas and jet tours (one class-specific detail per transition scene is enough — the rooms blur fast and readers skim).

---

## Ending patterns

- **The settlement**: the ending re-writes the contract in her words — she keeps her job, her name, her world, and adds him to it; the clause list becomes a love letter.
- **The ordinary-life anchor**: the final scene is her world (the diner, the ward, the neighborhood) with him in it, out of his depth, happy — the inversion made permanent.
- **The callback revalue**: the opening object (the receipt, the watch, the tart) returns, its meaning changed.
- **The second-contract**: a short epilogue scene one year later where she renegotiates — for the family, for the company, for him; she wins, he signs, done.
- HE is a hard constraint: the couple ends together with her dignity and her world intact. If the emotion goal is a walking-away ending, that's second-chance.md territory — decide in Phase 1 and don't switch.

## Genre-specific tuning (deviations from the general de-AI rules)

These read as AI-flavor elsewhere but are selling points here — **don't cut them**:

- **Class-anchored observation** (prices, brands, power signals with meaning): keep; it's the genre's texture. One signal per scene, not a catalogue.
- **Contract-language dialogue**: keep the terms-and-counters exchanges; the genre's romance happens in the margins of the document.
- **The competence reveal**: keep the working scenes in full; the heroine's skill is the genre's dignity engine.
- **The inversion beat**: keep the public, concrete leverage scene; never replace it with passive rescue.
- **The action grovel**: keep apology-by-action; delete grovel speeches.
- **Fish-out-of-water humor**: keep the dry narration of his world's absurdity; it's the reader's comfort.

Still avoid the genuine AI-flavor list: "the kind of X that Y", "a wave of X washed over", "his eyes were like stormy seas", "in that moment", "she felt a mix of X and Y", "the air crackled with tension" (show the negotiation instead), and the heroine being a passive prize — rescue without her leverage reads as dated and kills the genre's current market. **Also avoid**: making the heroine cringe at her own world (her dignity is the point), and the hero being cruel for sport with no counter.

## Structural formulas (by length)

- **The arrangement (8-11 sections)**: 1-2 collision + contract → 3-4 his-world/ her-world alternation, first-class lessons refused → 5 competence reveal → 6 family price + arrangement crisis → 7 the low point (worst clause) → 8 the inversion beat → 9-10 action grovel + surrender → HE. First person; one POV.
- **The revenge premise (8-11 sections)**: the wrong done years ago as the frame → her access (the PA job, the audit) → the double game (her plan vs. his attention) → the moment she can ruin him and doesn't (the low point) → the truth scene → the inversion becomes reconciliation. The revenge must be real (a document, a deal, a name) and her renunciation must cost her something.
- **The rescue-with-a-bill (8-10 sections)**: the debt frame → the working-off montage (one work scene per section) → the debt's hidden terms revealed → the inversion (she pays it off on her terms) → surrender + HE.

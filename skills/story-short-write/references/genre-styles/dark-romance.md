# Genre Style Pack: Dark Romance

> English short-form romance for Inkitt / Radish / Galatea / Dreame / Wattpad (mature sections). Read `short-craft.md` first; this pack layers the genre-specific voice on top.
> One-line positioning: **desire that arrives through danger, obsession, and morally grey choices — the love interest is a threat first and a partner second, and the reader is here for the controlled fall.** The reader wants the frisson of being wanted by someone who could destroy her, the line-crossing that is consensual and negotiated, and the hard-won safety at the end.

---

## Narrative voice

First person (the female lead) or close third per platform: Inkitt and Radish favor first person; Galatea's app-first drama often runs first too. The voice runs on **danger-sensing and forbidden noticing**: the narration tracks the threat with the same attention it tracks the attraction — "he was standing too close. I counted how fast I could get to the door. I didn't move." Internal monologue oscillates between survival logic and want; the want always arrives with a warning that she ignores on purpose.

Dual track: the **prey track** (alert, negotiating, boundary-holding) and the **predator track** (the love interest's POV, used sparingly and only after the midpoint — his control cracking is the reveal). The story's engine is consent made explicit and constant: the danger is real, the escalation is negotiated, and the line-crossing scenes always contain a choice the heroine makes with full information. This is the pack's ethical spine and its market edge — dark romance readers abandon books that blur into abuse narratives.

## Opening patterns

A 120-220 word blurb opens with the danger met + the first line of negotiation; the body's first section re-shoots the meet with texture.

| Opening type | Example |
|--------------|---------|
| The debt owed | "He didn't ask for the money back. He asked for me — a year, one night a week, his rules. I laughed until I saw his face. He'd never been joking." |
| The wrong door | "The apartment I sublet belonged to a dead woman. Her brother showed up at midnight holding her key, looked at my suitcase, and said, 'You have two minutes to explain who you are.' I took four." |
| The arranged meeting | "The contract said the marriage would protect my family's company. It didn't say the man signing it was the one the board whispered about — the one who'd buried three rivals and smiled through all three funerals." |
| The reaper's favor | "I worked for him for two years and never learned his real name. Then a rival took me, and I found out what he did to people who touched his staff. The problem is that knowing didn't make me want to leave." |
| The inheritance | "My grandmother left me a house and a warning: don't open the door to the man who comes for the rose garden. He came. The house was the only thing either of us wanted, and the garden was between us." |

## Hook motifs and density

The hook is **the price being paid and the line being held**: every section either raises the cost or re-negotiates the boundary. End sections on a threat that is also a promise.

- **The debt / contract** (head motif): a formal arrangement (debt, contract marriage, protection-for-services, witness protection) that keeps them legally entangled — the arrangement is the frame, the breaking of its rules is the romance.
- **The dangerous capability**: the love interest is competent at violence, money, or control — shown once early (the reader must believe the threat), then held in reserve; his restraint is the love language.
- **The line scene**: each escalation of intimacy comes with a spoken or acted boundary check — "Say the word and I stop." The check is the genre's signature; missing it turns the scene into abuse.
- **The third-party threat**: the true danger is never the love interest's heart — it's the rival, the family, the syndicate; he protects her from it while being the only safe place in the storm. (The "safe villain" paradox.)
- **The revealed wound**: his monstrosity has a cause (the sister he failed, the foster system, the betrayal that broke him) — revealed at 60-70%, humanizing without excusing.
- **The surrender of control**: her final choice is to let him in — and his final proof of love is giving her the exit (the documents, the money, the door) that he could have withheld.
- **The obsession clock**: his control cracks in measurable steps (late calls, broken rules, showing up uninvited) — the cracks are the romance's progress bar and the reader's thrill.

## Emotional intensity and patterns (intensity 9)

The engine is **controlled danger + negotiated intimacy**: the reader wants to feel unsafe in a safe container. Intensity comes from proximity, not gore — the darkest scenes are the ones where nothing violent happens and everything is at stake.

- The alert register: short, physical, present-tense-in-effect ("I could hear my own heartbeat. So could he. He smiled.").
- The want register: named outright, then landed on a body-specific tell — never "a wave of desire"; instead the shaking hand she lets him hold, the pulse he counts aloud.
- The boundary register: her lines are explicit and respected — "Not the neck." "Not tonight." — and the respect of the line is itself erotic; it is also the reader's trust anchor.
- The crack register (his POV, after midpoint): his control breaking reads as panic, not rage — the first time he says "I don't know how to stop wanting you" like it's a confession, not a threat.
- The low point: the third-party threat reaches her despite him (or through his family), and the blame scene happens — she blames him for making her safe in a world that isn't; the reconciliation is him burning the world down to make it true.
- Violence: present but gate-kept. On-page violence is never sexualized, never between them, and never gratuitous — his violence is shown once in full (opening capability scene), afterwards only its consequences.

Sample passage (register demonstration):

> He found me in the library. Of course he did. I'd told him once, weeks ago, that I studied there when I needed quiet, and he'd filed it away like everything else I said, like I was a case file he was building.
> "You're late," I said, without looking up.
> "I know." He sat across from me and put something on the table — a velvet box. "I'm asking this time."
> I closed my book. "Asking what?"
> "If I may court you like a normal man. Badly." He pushed the box toward me. It held a key. "To the apartment above my garage. Not the main house. You set the rules. I'll sign whatever you want."
> "Why?"
> "Because I don't want to be the man who takes. I'd rather be the man who earns, and I've never earned anything in my life." He said it like a diagnosis. "Teach me."
> The key sat between us, warm on the wood. I didn't touch it. I also didn't stand up and leave.

Second sample (the low point — the blame scene):

> They'd taken her because of him. That was the fact, and he stood in the doorway with his hands at his sides, letting the fact sit.
> "You said you'd keep me safe," I said. "That was the whole deal. That was the only clause that mattered."
> "I know."
> "She's nine, Eli. She was drawing in the garden. They took her because of a debt I didn't even know you still owed."
> "I know." His voice didn't shake. His hands were the giveaway — the hands that never shook, still at his sides, fingers gone white. "I've spent the last hour deciding which lie to tell you. There isn't one. I owe the truth, and the truth is worse."
> He told me. The room went very quiet, and somewhere in the quiet, I understood the difference between being afraid of a man and being afraid for him. The second one was worse. The second one was love.

## Dialogue register and killer lines

Dialogue ratio 40-55%. The power marker is negotiation: every exchange between them is a contract being drafted — he proposes terms, she counters, the final draft is the relationship.

**① The negotiation register**
- "Say the word and I stop." / "Which word?" / "Any word. You pick. I'll learn it."
- "I'm not asking for your trust. I'm asking for one hour, in a public place, where you can leave at any time. That's the entire offer."
- "You keep saying I can leave." / "Because you can. The door's not locked. It never has been. You just never checked."

**② The want register (threat as confession)**
- "I've done terrible things for less than this. You should be more careful with me." / "You're the one who should be careful. I've wanted you since the night I thought you'd died."
- "I don't chase. I wait. I've been waiting for you for four years, and I'm very good at waiting."
- "You're not afraid of me. That's the most dangerous thing about you."

**③ The boundary register (her lines, his respect)**
- "Not the neck." / "Noted." He kissed her collarbone like it was a negotiation he'd won. "Ask me for anything and I'll give it. That's the rule. I keep the rules I make."
- "If you lie to me, I'm gone. Not angry. Gone." / "Then you'll never be gone."

**④ The surrender register (the earned ending)**
- "I'm not surrendering. I'm choosing. There's a difference, and you're going to have to live with it."
- "The exit's still open," he said, holding the door. "It'll always be open. I just want you to know I never used it myself."

|------|-----------|---------|
| The debt/contract | Legal or formal entanglement as the frame | Contract marriage, debt, protection agreement |
| The capability scene | Show his danger once, fully, early | The rival's men, the courtroom, the boardroom |

| The safe villain paradox | He protects her from the world while being the world's danger | The stalker, the family, the syndicate |
| The revealed wound | His monstrosity's cause at 60-70%; humanize, don't excuse | The sister, the system, the betrayal |
| The obsession clock | His control cracks in measurable steps | Late calls, broken rules, uninvited arrivals |
| The exit gift | His final proof is the door he could have withheld | Documents, money, the key, the signed release |
| The public claim | His possessiveness goes public once, sanctioned by her | The gala, the family dinner, the rival's office |
| The blame scene | The low point where she names what he cost her | The kidnapping, the family lie, the missed warning |
| The earned safety | The ending is domesticated danger: still him, now hers | The garage apartment, the rules they both signed |

## Rhythm skeleton

```
Blurb (danger met + first negotiation line)
→ Section 1: the meet re-shot; the capability scene or its shadow
→ The arrangement locks in (debt/contract/arranged meeting)
→ Escalation cycles: one line scene + one crack per section
→ The revealed wound (60-70%); his POV chapter (optional, post-midpoint)
→ Third-party threat lands; the blame scene
→ The exit gift + the choice beat
→ Earned safety + HE (domesticated danger)
```

Dense in the line scenes and the blame scene; sparse in between. The violence stays in its one early scene plus consequences; every escalation is negotiated on the page.

---

## Ending patterns

- **The earned safety**: the ending is a settled, bounded domesticity — the garage apartment, the signed rules, the morning routine — where his danger is still real and pointed elsewhere. HE with an edge.
- **The exit honored**: she takes the exit once, he honors it, and she comes back on her own terms — the return is the proof.
- **The public settlement**: the final scene is him handling the last third-party threat publicly, with her beside him, unafraid, correcting his negotiating style.
- **The callback line**: the opening threat ("say the word") returns as the closing tenderness ("say the word and I stay").
- HE is a hard constraint for this pack's main line: the couple ends safe and chosen. Dark endings (she leaves, he lets her) are a valid second branch — decide from the Phase 1 emotion goal and never bait-and-switch: if the blurb promises the fall, deliver the landing.

## Genre-specific tuning (deviations from the general de-AI rules)

These read as AI-flavor elsewhere but are selling points here — **don't cut them**:

- **Danger-adjacent sensory narration** ("I could hear my own heartbeat. So could he."): keep; proximity is the genre's intensity.
- **Negotiation dialogue**: keep the contract-drafting exchanges; they are the genre's signature and its safety mechanism.
- **The possessiveness with boundaries**: keep the public claim and the obsession cracks — but always paired with the spoken, respected line. Possession without consent is abuse content and a platform-ban risk; the line scene is non-negotiable.
- **His POV crack chapter**: keep one, post-midpoint, short; his control breaking is the reveal.
- **The revealed wound**: keep the cause of his darkness; don't sand it into a redemption speech.
- **Blame scene directness**: keep her naming the cost; it's the genre's emotional climax.

Still avoid the genuine AI-flavor list: "the kind of X that Y", "a wave of X washed over", "his gaze pierced", "in that moment", "eyes darkened with desire" (show the negotiation instead), "couldn't help but", and any scene where intimacy happens without a boundary check. **Platform content lines**: keep on-page sex tasteful-to-implied on Wattpad/Tapas, explicit-on-request only for Inkitt/Radish/Galatea/Dreame where the platform allows; never write sexualized violence or non-consent as titillation — those are the genre's bright lines and the difference between dark romance and abuse content.

## Structural formulas (by length)

- **The debt/contract (8-11 sections)**: 1-2 the meet + the arrangement → 3-4 first line scenes, capability in reserve → 5-6 escalation + obsession cracks → 7 the revealed wound → 8 third-party threat + blame scene → 9 the exit gift + choice → 10 earned safety. First person; his POV chapter optional at 65%.
- **The arranged marriage (8-10 sections)**: contract frame → the boardroom/family pressure as the antagonist → public claim + negotiation scenes → the truth about why he chose her (the revealed wound) → the family's counter-move → the choice beat → HE.
- **The safe-villain thriller hybrid (10-13 sections)**: the romance and the thriller share the engine — each danger escalation is also an intimacy beat; the third-party threat is the plot's spine. The genre pack for pacing scaffolds is psychological-thriller.md; the romance mechanics stay here.

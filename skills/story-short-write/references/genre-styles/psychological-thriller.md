# Genre Style Pack: Psychological Thriller

> English short-form suspense for Inkitt / Galatea / Radish / Kindle short reads / Wattpad. Read `short-craft.md` first; this pack layers the genre-specific voice on top. Suspense mechanics (information order, layered hooks, expectation relay, time locks) live in `hooks-suspense.md`; this pack covers voice, structure, and pattern.
> One-line positioning: **the danger is a mind — gaslighting, obsession, a narrator who can't trust her own memory, a truth that re-arranges everything already read.** The reader wants the vertigo of not knowing who to believe, the dread that creeps rather than jumps, and an ending that makes them flip back to page one.

---

## Narrative voice

First person (the unreliable or targeted narrator) or close third (the detective/dual-track) per platform: Inkitt and Radish favor first person; Galatea runs both. The voice runs on **calm reportage of abnormal things**: the narrator states the unbelievable in flat, precise language, and the horror lives in the gap between tone and content. Interiority is analytical, not emotive — the narrator reasons her way toward the truth while the reader watches her reasons bend.

Two tracks: the **target track** (the narrator who is being gaslit, erased, or hunted — the reader's sympathies) and the **operator track** (the gaslighter, the stalker, the twin, the "helpful" friend — revealed through their controlled scenes or through the target's growing recognition). Dual POV is allowed (Inkitt/Galatea normal); the operator's POV must never confirm or deny the target's reality until the reveal — ambiguity is the engine.

**Narrative rules**: the narrator may omit, but may not lie to the reader — everything narrated is what she believes; the correction comes from new information, never from "I was lying all along." If the narrator is herself the operator (the unreliable reveal), her omissions must be re-readable as misdirection, not cheating.

## Opening patterns

A 120-220 word blurb opens with the abnormal detail + one line of unease; the body's first section re-shoots it.

| Opening type | Example |
|--------------|---------|
| The too-clean life | "My husband is perfect. That's the first thing everyone says, and it's true: he remembers everything, he's never late, and he's already filed the missing-person report I didn't know existed. He filed it three days before I disappeared. He's so good at this." |
| The erased existence | "The pharmacy had no record of my prescription. The pharmacy had no record of me. My name wasn't in the system, my face wasn't on the license, and the clerk asked if I was sure I lived here. I've lived here for eleven years. So has the man watching me from the end of the aisle." |
| The helpful friend | "When I woke up in the hospital, my best friend was the one who told me I'd had an accident. She was so calm, so kind, so certain. She also had my phone, my keys, and my apartment key. She said she was keeping them safe. Safe from what, she didn't say." |
| The diary that knows too much | "I found a diary in my own handwriting. It's dated next week. It knows things I haven't done yet, names I haven't met, and one entry that says 'he knows about the wall. don't let him find the wall.' I don't have a wall. I checked." |
| The twin | "My sister died five years ago. That's what everyone told me, and I believed them, because I was at the funeral. Then she called me from a payphone at the gas station across the street and asked if I'd buried the right person." |

## Hook motifs and density

The hook is **the expanding gap between what the narrator knows and what's true**: every section either adds a new impossible detail or re-explains an old one. End sections on a detail that re-arranges the previous reading.

- **The coordinated denial** (head motif): everyone the narrator trusts tells her the same story — she's confused, she's stressed, she misremembers. The denial's coordination is the clue; the moment one of them slips is the crack.
- **The impossible detail**: one small, verifiable, unexplainable fact (the wrong date on the receipt, the photo with an extra person, the door unlocked from the inside). Concrete and repeatable — the reader must be able to check it.
- **The micro-evidence**: the tiny physical trace that survives the gaslighting (the scratch on the frame, the sand in the shoe, the voicemail length). Collected, catalogued, re-examined — the narrator's evidence board is her sanity's scaffolding.
- **The helpful operator**: the gaslighter is charming, competent, and always first to help — the "concerned" spouse, the "supportive" friend, the "attentive" therapist. Their help is the trap; their one slip is the crack.
- **The witness who won't say**: one person knows the truth and won't speak (the neighbor, the nurse, the child) — their silence and its reason is a mid-story reveal.
- **The re-arranged past**: a memory the narrator was sure of is re-verified and wrong — the correction is either proof of gaslighting or proof of her unreliability; the reader must stay unsure which.
- **The time lock**: a deadline pressure (the sale date, the surgery, the trip, the birthday) that makes inaction impossible — the clock is the operator's or the narrator's, and its expiry is the climax.
- **The trust-betrayal ladder**: each section, the narrator's support system loses one person (the therapist moves, the friend "takes a break," the husband's brother stops returning calls) — isolation as plotted progress.

## Emotional intensity and patterns (intensity 8)

The engine is **dread through reason**: the narrator stays analytical, so the reader's fear is a half-step ahead of hers. Emotion is written as physical certainty (the wrong pulse, the cold, the count) rather than named feelings; fear never gets a "wave."

- The reportage register: flat, precise, comma-long sentences; the abnormal narrated like inventory.
- The certainty register: the moments the narrator is briefly sure — her confidence is the reader's alarm bell.
- The doubt register: the sentence that folds ("unless," "but," "I checked twice") — each fold is a small vertigo hit.
- The physical register: fear lands in body details specific to the scene (the hand that counts the pulse, the shoe full of sand, the missed heartbeat on the recording) — one per beat, never stacked.
- The crack register: the operator's single slip (the wrong name, the invented memory, the "I didn't say that") — narrated in the target's flat voice, which makes it land harder.
- The collapse: the narrator's lowest point is her own unreliability confirmed (the memory corrected, the evidence gone, the witness recanting) — one scene, full, then the recovery begins with the micro-evidence that survived.
- Violence: present but restrained — the genre's payoff is the reveal, not the action; on-page violence is brief and functional (the confrontation, the escape), never pornographic.

Sample passage (register demonstration):

> The recording was seven seconds long. I'd played it forty-one times, so I knew it by heart: the sound of my own voice asking him where he'd been, and his voice answering, calm and patient, that he'd been home all evening, and hadn't I just seen him in the kitchen.
> The seventh second was the problem. There was a click before his answer — not a phone noise, a door noise. The same click my front door made when it closed from the outside.
> I played it once more with the volume at maximum, holding the phone in both hands like evidence in a trial.
> He came in from the garage, set down his keys, and smiled at me the way he always did — the way that made everyone say he was so patient with me.
> "You're up late," he said.
> "Just listening to something," I said. "Nothing important."
> The click was still playing in my head. He'd been home all evening. The door had closed from the outside. One of those things was a lie, and I had seven seconds of proof, and he was smiling at me like I was the one who couldn't be trusted.

Second sample (the collapse — her own reliability confirmed broken):

> The pharmacy had no record of my prescription. That was the fourth time this week a record of me had quietly not existed, and I'd come prepared: I brought the bottle. The pharmacist held it up to the light and said the label looked right, and then he said the thing that broke something in my chest.
> "But this isn't your prescription, is it? The name on it — Anna. This says Anna."
> I took the bottle back and read it. It said my name. It said my name, I would have sworn it, and it said Anna, and my hands were shaking the way they'd shaken the first week, before I started writing everything down.
> I drove home slowly, reciting facts to myself the way he'd taught me — the date, the route, my address — and when I got to the kitchen, the evidence was exactly where I'd hidden it: the sand in the shoe, the recording, the photograph with the extra person.
> The photograph was the worst. I'd written the date on the back, in my handwriting. The date was next week.
> He came in from the garage, set down his keys, and asked if I'd had a good day. I said yes. I said it the way I'd practiced, calm and small, and I thought: if I'm wrong, I'm losing my mind. And if I'm right, I'm married to the man who's taking it from me. Either way, I'm not safe here.

## Dialogue register and killer lines

Dialogue ratio 40-55%. The power marker is who confirms reality: the operator speaks in calm, complete, reality-defining sentences; the narrator's lines get shorter and more careful as she learns to stop handing him information.

**① The operator register (calm, reality-defining)**
- "You're exhausted. You've been under so much strain. I'll handle the arrangements — you just rest."
- "I don't know why you keep bringing this up. We talked about it. You agreed it was nothing."
- "It's the medication. The doctor said it could affect your memory. You trust the doctor, don't you?"
- "I never said that. I would never say that. You're confusing me with someone else."

**② The target register (shortening, careful)**
- "Okay."
- "I must have been wrong."
- "Nothing. I was thinking about nothing."
- "You're right. It was probably nothing." (The reader hears the lie she's telling him — her first victory.)

**③ The witness register (won't say, says too little)**
- "Ask your mother about the summer you were twelve." / "She says it never happened." / "Then you've got your answer, haven't you?"
- "I don't know anything. I just know he's very good at being believed."

**④ The reveal register (the evidence walk)**
- "The diary knew about the wall because I wrote it. Not last week. Three years ago, in the hospital, when he was already here. The wall was the safe part of the story — the part he couldn't take."
- "You asked if I'd buried the right person. I did. That's the problem. I buried her, and she came back to ask me why."

## Rhythm skeleton

```
Blurb (abnormal detail + one line of unease)
→ Section 1: the detail lands; the narrator establishes her reality
→ The denial coordinates; first slip (crack) around 25-30%
→ Impossible details accumulate; micro-evidence collected (one per section)
→ The witness who won't say; the re-arranged past (midpoint vertigo)
→ The trust-betrayal ladder; time lock starts ticking
→ The collapse: her own reliability confirmed broken
→ The recovery: surviving micro-evidence re-examined
→ The reveal (re-readable; the reader flips back)
→ Aftermath: one scene, quiet, the new truth settling
```

Dense in the crack scenes and the reveal; sparse in the evidence-cataloguing (one detail per section is enough — a catalogue reads as padding). Every section must end on a re-arrangement or a clock tick.

---

## Ending patterns

- **The re-readable reveal**: the truth re-arranges everything already read; the final scene echoes the opening scene with its meaning inverted. The reader must be able to flip back and find the seams.
- **The quiet victory**: the narrator escapes with the surviving evidence, one scene, restrained — no monologue explaining the whole plot; the reader assembles it.
- **The operator's slip**: the ending is the operator's first full crack — caught by the narrator's evidence, in public or in private, with the reader watching their composure finally fail.
- **The open dread**: the narrator is free but one small detail suggests the pattern continues (the neighbor's new friend, the unclaimed message) — used only when the Phase 1 emotion goal is creeping dread; never as a lazy sequel hook that cheapens the resolution.
- Two valid closers: **confirmed** (the narrator was right; the operator convicted/exposed) and **ambiguous** (the reader still can't be fully certain — the last line re-opens the doubt). Decide from the emotion goal; if you promise a reveal, deliver it.

## Genre-specific tuning (deviations from the general de-AI rules)

These read as AI-flavor elsewhere but are selling points here — **don't cut them**:

- **Flat reportage of abnormal events**: keep the calm inventory voice; never polish it into emotive prose — the tone-content gap is the genre's dread engine.
- **Keyword-exact evidence**: the micro-evidence's wording must repeat identically at every appearance (the click, the sand, the seven seconds); synonym-rotation breaks the reader's ability to track it.
- **Repetition as pattern**: the operator's repeated phrases ("you're exhausted," "you agreed") are deliberate gaslighting texture, not redundant dialogue — keep them and make the count meaningful.
- **The re-arranged past**: keep the corrected memory scenes; they are the vertigo engine.
- **Chapter-end self-questions**: "Who was in the kitchen, then?" — keep as information-gap hooks; cut empty rhetorical questions that neither advance nor re-arrange.
- **The narrator's omissions**: keep the honest-omission narration; the reveal must be re-readable, never a gotcha.

Still avoid the genuine AI-flavor list: "the kind of X that Y", "a chill ran down her spine" (fear goes through physical certainty here, one detail per beat), "in that moment she knew" (the narrator's knowing must be earned on-page), "little did she know", "everything was about to change", and the operator being twirlable-cartoon evil — the gaslighter must be believable enough that the reader briefly doubted the narrator too.

## Structural formulas (by length)

- **The gaslit target (9-12 sections)**: 1-2 the impossible detail + reality established → 3-5 denial coordinates, first slip → 6 midpoint: witness who won't say, re-arranged past → 7-8 trust-betrayal ladder + time lock → 9 the collapse → 10-11 recovery via surviving micro-evidence + reveal → 12 aftermath. First person; the operator's POV optional, post-midpoint, never reality-confirming.
- **The dual-track hunt (10-13 sections)**: alternating target/operator (or detective/operator) POVs; the operator's chapters are controlled and informative but never confirm the target's reality; the reveal happens when the two tracks converge on the same scene. Allowed on Inkitt/Galatea; Wattpad serials prefer single-POV.
- **The doppelganger/infiltration (8-11 sections)**: the twin/double premise — identity replacement as the frame; the micro-evidence is the seam (the tattoo, the scar, the habit); the reveal is the identity's uncracked detail. Doubles as horror-adjacent; keep the mechanics here, the dread texture in horror.md.

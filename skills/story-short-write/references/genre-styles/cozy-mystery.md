# Genre Style Pack: Cozy Mystery

> English short-form mystery for Kindle short reads / Wattpad / Inkitt / Tapas. Read `short-craft.md` first; this pack layers the genre-specific voice on top. Suspense mechanics (information order, layered hooks, expectation relay) live in `hooks-suspense.md`; this pack covers voice, structure, and pattern.
> One-line positioning: **a likable amateur sleuth solves a death in a small, quirky community — the murder is off-page, the stakes are social, and the fun is the puzzle plus the people.** The reader wants the satisfaction of spotting the clue before the sleuth does, the comfort of the community, and a culprit who was always in the frame but never obvious.

---

## Narrative voice

First person (the amateur sleuth: the baker, the bookshop owner, the innkeeper, the retired librarian, the wedding planner) or close third. The voice runs on **warm curiosity**: the sleuth is nosy in a kind way, notices everything, and narrates the community with affection and gentle satire. Horror and gore stay off-page — the murder is already done, the body is handled respectfully (or comically), and the tension is intellectual and social.

Two tracks: the **observation track** (clues, inconsistencies, the town's small lies) and the **tea-and-sympathy track** (comfort rituals, community scenes, the suspect's sadness). The cozy tone works because the sleuth treats the investigation like hospitality: everyone gets fed, everyone gets questioned kindly, and the truth, when it lands, lands without cruelty to the reader.

---

## Opening patterns

A 120-220 word blurb opens with the body/discovery + one quirky hook; the body's first section re-shoots the discovery.

| Opening type | Example |
|--------------|---------|
| The delivery | "I was delivering the wedding cake when I found the groom dead in the pantry, clutching a fork and a very revealing shopping list. The bride asked if I could do the reception sandwiches anyway. Everyone was hungry." |
| The book return | "The victim checked out a library book the day she died: 'How to Hide a Body in Three Easy Steps.' It was overdue. So was she, apparently, in the gardening shed." |
| The festival setup | "The pie contest had three entries and one dead judge. The coroner said poison. The town said sabotage. I said the rhubarb entry had looked suspicious from the start." |
| The guest | "The inn's only guest that week was a man who paid in cash and asked about the well. By Friday, he was in the well. By Saturday, I was running the investigation from the front desk, because the sheriff had jury duty." |
| The inheritance | "My great-aunt left me her bakery and, taped under the flour bin, a note: 'If I die before the county fair, check the shortbread.' She died before the county fair. The shortbread was gone. So was the pharmacist." |

## Hook motifs and density

The hook is **the accumulating inconsistency**: every section adds one small wrong thing (the too-neat alibi, the mislabeled jar, the second cup of tea nobody ordered). End sections on a new inconsistency or a suspect's micro-reaction.

- **The closed circle** (head motif): the suspects are the community — the mayor, the rival baker, the volunteer fire chief, the gardening club — and the victim is someone everyone knew (and someone had a reason). The circle shrinks as sections pass.
- **The small lie**: someone is lying about something small (a receipt, a schedule, a phone call); the small lie is the thread that unravels the big truth.
- **The clue in plain sight**: the decisive clue appears early, in a social scene (the pie crust, the book spine, the sweater's missing button) and is only re-noticed at the reveal. It must be re-noticeable — same wording, same object.
- **The red herring with a reason**: the decoy suspect is lying, but about something human (the affair, the debt, the kindness) — never about nothing; the decoy's secret is the reader's "ah" at the reveal.
- **The competent sidekick**: the sheriff/constable is warm but slow, the best friend is smarter than she looks, the cat/knitting circle "helps." The sleuth is never alone in the investigation.
- **The social stakes**: the cost of being wrong is community rupture (the festival, the wedding, the shop boycott), not physical danger — the sleuth's urgency is social.
- **The tea-and-sympathy beat**: after every major discovery, a comfort scene (tea, walk, gossip with the sidekick) — the cozy rhythm; tension never runs two sections without a warm landing.
- **The drawing-room reveal**: the final scene gathers the suspects (the community event is the natural stage) and the sleuth walks through the evidence — the classic payoff; each suspect reacts in turn.

## Emotional intensity and patterns (intensity 4-5)

The engine is **gentle tension with cozy landings**: the danger is never the sleuth's life (the "amateur in peril" threat, if used at all, is a single scene and resolved without real violence). Emotion is named lightly and landed on comfort objects (the kettle, the afghan, the counter stool).

- The curiosity register: delighted noticing, "hmm" energy, the joy of the puzzle.
- The community register: affection and satire mixed — the town's absurdity is narrated warmly.
- The concern register: the sleuth's worry for the victim's family and the wrongly accused; the culprit is never cartoonish evil — the motive is human (greed, shame, protection) and the arrest is sad, not triumphant.
- The tension register: the one genuine danger beat (the threatened shop, the midnight intruder) is short and immediately re-contained.
- The reveal register: the satisfaction is structural — the reader's "I should have seen the jar" — delivered by the sleuth walking the evidence, not by surprise violence.

Sample passage (register demonstration):

> The sheriff held the jar up to the light like it was a suspect. "You're telling me this is the murder weapon?"
> "It's the container," I said. "The shortbread was the weapon. Someone swapped the sugar jar in my great-aunt's pantry. The one labeled SUGAR, I mean. The one that actually had sugar in it was labeled FLOUR. My aunt kept everything labeled wrong on purpose. She said it kept the help honest."
> "And who would know that?"
> "Anyone who ever borrowed flour from her." I set the second jar down next to the first. "Both jars are full. The flour jar's lighter. Someone refilled it in a hurry and put the label back crooked. The label's the clue. So is the apology."
> "What apology?"
> "Someone left a rhubarb pie on my doorstep this morning," I said. "I didn't order a pie. Nobody in this town bakes rhubarb except two people, and one of them's dead."

Second sample (the reveal — the evidence walk):

> The town hall was packed for the festival meeting, which meant the whole town was packed, which was exactly what I'd hoped for.
> "I'm not the committee chair," I said, from the front. "I just found something in the pantry." I set the two jars on the table, side by side. "The sugar jar is full. The flour jar is full. They're both labeled wrong, because my aunt labeled everything wrong on purpose. Someone who knew that borrowed the flour, used it to bake the shortbread, and refilled the jar in a hurry — look at the label. It's crooked."
> The room went quiet the way rooms go quiet when everyone's doing the same arithmetic.
> "The person who refilled it also apologized." I pulled the rhubarb pie out of my bag. "They left this on my doorstep. I didn't order it, and there are only two rhubarb bakers in town."
> Every head turned. Both bakers were at the meeting, one on each side of the aisle.
> "I didn't bake that pie," said the first.
> "Neither did I," said the second, too fast.
> I smiled at the second one. "I know. That's the problem. The pie's from the bakery on Main Street, and the bakery's been closed since Tuesday." I set it down gently. "Whoever left it wanted to look like an apology. What it really is, is a receipt."

## Dialogue register and killer lines

Dialogue ratio 50-65%. The power marker is indirectness: suspects speak politely and say less than they know; the sleuth asks questions that sound like hospitality.

**① The social register (suspects being agreeable)**
- "I hardly knew her. We were in the gardening club together, but that's not knowing, is it?"
- "The festival committee would never let a disagreement go that far. We're professionals."
- "I was home with a migraine. Ask anyone. The whole street heard about the migraine."

**② The sleuth register (questions as kindness)**
- "You mentioned the sugar was a special order. Was that before or after the flour shipment went missing?"
- "I'm not suggesting anything. I'm just asking who else knew she labeled everything wrong. The way I see it, that's a short list, and the list is the story."

**③ The sidekick register (warm, sharper than she looks)**
- "The mayor's alibi is the Rotary meeting. The Rotary meeting was canceled. He didn't know that, because he wasn't there. That's not a clue, that's a gift."
- "You keep feeding suspects pie and calling it questioning. I keep coming along because the pie's excellent."

**④ The reveal register (evidence walk, no cruelty)**
- "The murderer is the one person who didn't ask about the sugar. Everyone else asked, because everyone else borrowed flour. The one who didn't ask already knew the pantry was a trap."
- "It wasn't hate. It was a debt, and the debt was the thing in the well."

| The tea-and-sympathy beat | A comfort scene after every major discovery | The kettle, the walk, the gossip |

| The mislabeled object | The community's private system is the clue | The wrong labels, the borrowed flour |
| The human motive | The culprit is sad, not evil; the arrest is quiet | Greed, shame, protection |

## Rhythm skeleton

```
Blurb (discovery + one quirky hook)
→ Section 1: the discovery re-shot; the community establishes
→ The closed circle: suspects introduced socially (one per section, each with a small lie)
→ Inconsistencies accumulate; red herring's human secret surfaces
→ Midpoint: the clue in plain sight is re-noticed; a wrong accusation nearly sticks
→ The single danger beat (short, re-contained); social stakes raised
→ The drawing-room reveal: evidence walked, suspects react
→ Quiet landing: the community grieves, the sleuth serves tea
```

Dense in the social scenes and the reveal; sparse in transitions. The clue-in-plain-sight wording must repeat exactly at the reveal — the reader's recognition is the payoff.

---

## Ending patterns

- **The drawing-room reveal**: all suspects gathered (a community event), the sleuth walks the evidence, each suspect reacts — the culprit's reaction is the quietest.
- **The social repair**: the ending restores the community — the wrongly accused cleared in public, the festival saved, the culprit's family held by the same kindness that caught them.
- **The tea-and-truth callback**: the opening comfort image (the pie, the book, the counter stool) returns in the last line, revalued.
- **The next-case tease**: one line hints the sleuth's services will be needed again — a new customer, a new box, a new label. Optional; keep it to one line.

## Genre-specific tuning (deviations from the general de-AI rules)

These read as AI-flavor elsewhere but are selling points here — **don't cut them**:

- **Hospitality-framed questioning**: keep the sleuth's questions-as-kindness; it's the genre's signature and its tone anchor.
- **Comfort rituals**: keep the tea/pie/walk beats in full; the cozy rhythm is the genre.
- **Warm satire of the town**: keep the affection-and-satire narration; never flatten into neutral description.
- **The humanized culprit**: keep the motive human and the arrest quiet; a cartoon villain breaks the cozy contract.
- **Clue wording repetition**: keep the exact same wording for the clue at first sight and at the reveal — the reader's recognition is the payoff; synonym-rotating it breaks the puzzle.
- **Off-page violence**: keep the death and any danger off-page or instantly re-contained; graphic detail is a genre violation.

Still avoid the genuine AI-flavor list: "the kind of X that Y", "a chill ran down her spine" (the genre runs warm), "in that moment", "she knew instantly" (the sleuth earns every deduction on-page), "little did she know", and the detective being omniscient — the sleuth must miss things and be corrected by the sidekick at least once.

## Structural formulas (by length)

- **The community murder (8-11 sections)**: 1-2 discovery + circle established → 3-5 suspects introduced one per section, small lies accumulate → 6 midpoint: clue re-noticed, wrong accusation nearly sticks → 7 the single danger beat + social stakes → 8-9 drawing-room reveal → 10 quiet landing. First person; the sidekick as the sounding board.
- **The locked community event (8-10 sections)**: the festival/wedding/fair as the closed frame — every suspect was on the grounds; the schedule and the food are the evidence map; the reveal happens on the event's second day (the judges' table, the bridal suite).
- **The inheritance trap (7-10 sections)**: the victim left a system (labels, codes, a will with clauses); each clue is one part of the system; the final clue names the culprit in the will's last clause. Merges cozy mechanics with a light will-reading reveal.

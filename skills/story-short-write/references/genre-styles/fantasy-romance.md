# Genre Style Pack: Fantasy Romance

> English short-form romance for Wattpad / Tapas / Inkitt / Galatea / Kindle short reads. Read `short-craft.md` first; this pack layers the genre-specific voice on top.
> One-line positioning: **a world with rules and a love that has to beat them — fated mates, courts and curses, portals and chosen ones, where the magic is the obstacle and the bond is the lever.** The reader wants the immersion of a real magic system, the stakes of a world that punishes love, and the couple who save each other by being exactly who they are.

---

## Narrative voice

First person or close third per platform (Wattpad/Tapas YA is first person; Inkitt/Galatea adult fantasy varies). The voice runs on **world-competent observation**: the narrator knows the magic's rules the way a local knows traffic, and narrates the fantastical with the same concrete eye used for prices and weather. Exposition never arrives as a lesson — the rules surface through use, cost, and consequence.

Dual track: the **mortal track** (the human/ordinary lead discovering the world) and the **fae/sorcerer track** (the magical lead bound by the world's rules — the bond, the curse, the crown). The tension is between the world's law and the heart's claim: every romance beat is also a rule beaten. Magic has a cost (the fae cannot lie but bargain in words; the curse takes a year of memory per use; the bond burns when they're apart) — the cost is the genre's obstacle engine and its emotional lever.

---

## Opening patterns

A 120-220 word blurb opens with the premise's collision (the bond, the portal, the curse) + one rule that hurts; the body's first section re-shoots the collision.

| Opening type | Example |
|--------------|---------|
| The bond | "The binding took hold at midnight, in the king's hall, in front of everyone who mattered: I, a hedge witch with no name, was now soul-bound to the prince who'd had my village's crops burned. The binding said we'd die if we were apart. It said nothing about how much we'd want to." |
| The portal | "The door at the back of the bookshop wasn't there yesterday. It was there today, and it opened onto a forest that smelled like my grandmother's kitchen, and a man with silver eyes was waiting with a lantern and an apology: 'You were supposed to come through sixty years ago. I've been holding the door.'" |
| The curse | "The curse on our family was simple: the eldest daughter loves once, and the love is taken. I was the eldest. I knew the rules. I met him anyway, at the harvest fair, and I felt the curse wake up like a dog at the door." |
| The court | "The fae court doesn't invite humans to their masque. They summon them. I was summoned because my grandmother once owed a debt, and the queen collects debts with interest — and she collects interest with marriages." |
| The chosen wrong one | "The prophecy said a warrior would wake the sleeping king and break the frost. It didn't say the warrior was a baker. It definitely didn't say the baker was from the wrong kingdom, with the wrong banner on her coat and the right amount of spite." |

## Hook motifs and density

The hook is **the rule and its cost**: every section either adds a rule-learned-the-hard-way, raises the cost of the bond, or advances the world-threat. End sections on a rule breaking, a cost arriving, or a bond-ache.

- **The binding / bond** (head motif): a magical or legal bond (soul-bond, marriage-of-law, blood-debt) that ties them together and hurts when it's tested — the bond is the forced proximity of the fantasy register, and its unraveling is the romance's progress.
- **The fae bargain**: fae can't lie but trade in words — every negotiation is a contract scene with a loophole; the loophole discovered and used is a signature payoff.
- **The world-threat clock**: the frost, the blight, the invading court, the dying magic — the plot's countdown; the romance and the mission are the same task (the bond is the key, the key is the threat).
- **The magic with a cost**: each use of the magic costs something concrete (memory, years, blood, the season's warmth) — the cost scenes are the sacrifice beats; the couple's willingness to pay each other's cost is the love language.
- **The court as antagonist**: the politics (the queen, the council, the rival houses) is the world's pressure; the couple beats it with the world's own rules (the bargain, the prophecy, the loophole).
- **The mistaken identity within the bond**: the bond/cursed one is not who they seemed (the enemy is the ally, the servant is the heir, the baker is the warrior) — the identity reveal re-routes the prior friction into wonder or guilt.
- **The bond-ache beat**: the physical cost of separation (the burning distance, the shared pain) — one scene per act, used as the chapter-end hook.
- **The prophecy twist**: the prophecy said one thing and meant another — the twist lands at 75-85% and re-defines the mission, never invalidating it.

## Emotional intensity and patterns (intensity 7)

The engine is **world-scale stakes, intimate feelings**: the magic makes the stakes huge, but the emotion stays concrete and small (the shared fire, the mended sleeve, the name finally said aloud). Emotion is named and landed on world-specific objects (the bond-thread, the curse-mark, the lantern).

- The wonder register: the narrator's delight in the world's specifics (the door, the forest, the first spell that works) — one concrete wonder per arrival scene, then back to business.
- The cost register: the magic's price paid in scenes, concrete (the memory gone, the years taken, the season cold) — the cost is never abstracted.
- The bond register: the ache of distance, the warmth of nearness, written physical (the thread pulling, the cold in the chest, the shared heartbeat) — the genre's intimacy language.
- The court register: the politics as pressure — the queen's smile, the council's vote, the rival's betrothal — each scene a move in the contract game.
- The low point: the world-threat or the court forces the separation (the bargain broken, the bond cut, the exile) and the couple faces the cost of choosing each other — the sacrifice beat.
- The payoff: the couple wins by being exactly who they are (the baker's spite, the fae's honesty, the loophole found by the mortal) — the identity-as-weapon finale; the magic cooperates because they did.

Sample passage (register demonstration):

> The bond woke me at dawn with a tug, like a cat at the hem of a blanket. He was in the courtyard. I could feel him the way you feel weather — pressure, then the change.
> "You're late," I said, when I found him by the well. The frost was worse this morning; his breath hung in the air like a held sentence.
> "The queen summoned me." He said it like an apology.
> "And?"
> "And she wants the bond broken. She's found a loophole." He looked at me, and the tug in my chest pulled harder. "It requires one of us to die."
> "Cheap," I said. "For a queen."
> "It's not the queen I'm worried about." He took my hand — the gesture cost him; the frost crawled up his wrist, and he didn't let go. "It's that you'll say yes, to save the village."
> I looked at his wrist, at the frost, at the man who'd burned my crops and then spent a winter learning to plant them better.
> "The village is saved," I said. "The frost is the queen's. The bond is ours. Let her find a loophole in that."
> The tug in my chest pulled once, twice, and then settled, like a thing that had decided to stay.

Second sample (the payoff — the loophole wedding):

> The queen's court gathered at dusk for the reading of the contract. She read it herself, savoring every clause, and when she reached the end she smiled the smile that had ended wars.
> "The bond is void by the terms," she said. "One of you must die, or the frost takes the village by morning. Choose, and choose quickly."
> I stepped forward. The court went silent — that particular silence reserved for people who smile at queens.
> "Your Majesty reads the contract well," I said. "Allow me to read it back to you. Clause three: the bond is void if either party dies. Clause seven: the bond is void if the debt is paid in the coin of the realm. And the rider, your own rider, appended by your own hand: 'the coin of the realm includes the heart of its sovereign.'"
> The queen's smile didn't move. That was how the court knew she'd been beaten.
> "I don't want your heart," I said. "I want your signature. The frost ends, the village is spared, and the bond —" I held up the paper I'd been holding since the reading began. "The bond is void. My baker's license, witnessed by your own council, at the harvest fair, before the bond ever took. I was already bound. The contract never applied."
> He was looking at me the way he'd looked at me the first morning — like weather, like a change in the pressure.
> "You knew," he said. "You knew the whole time."
> "I know contracts," I said. "I bake bread. The two trades are the same: you can't rush the rising."

## Dialogue register and killer lines

Dialogue ratio 40-55%. The power marker is contract speech: fae and courts speak in precise, loophole-ready sentences; mortals answer in plain speech that the magic can't twist — plainness is the mortal's power move.

**① The bargain register (fae/court precision)**
- "You asked for your village spared. Spared it is. You did not ask for it spared from me."
- "I cannot lie, so I will say it plainly: I would burn the court for you. I have not. That is the largest truth I can offer."
- "The bond is death, the bargain is debt, and the debt is due. Choose your clause."

**② The plain-mortal register (the loophole-finder)**
- "You said the debt is due. You didn't say who collects it. The baker collects it now."
- "I don't bargain in words. I bargain in bread. The village ate yours for a year. You owe us."

**③ The bond register (intimacy under the world's pressure)**
- "The distance hurts. I didn't want to tell you, because it sounds like need." / "It is need. It's the only honest thing the magic does."
- "If the frost takes everything, I want it to take me knowing you chose the village and still came back."

**④ The payoff register (identity as weapon)**
- "The prophecy said a warrior would wake the king. It didn't say what kind. I'm the kind who bakes the king's favorite bread and knows exactly where the royal kitchens hide the good flour. Wake up, Your Majesty. The frost is hungry."
- "The queen collects debts. Good. I collected hers. Her favorite courtier owes me a horse, a favor, and a lie about the north gate. The north gate will be open."

## Rhythm skeleton

```
Blurb (premise collision + one rule that hurts)
→ Section 1: the collision re-shot; the world's rule stated in use
→ The bond/mission established; one cost paid per act
→ Bargain and court scenes (one contract per section); bond-ache beats
→ The world-threat escalates; the mistaken identity reveals
→ The low point: the forced separation / the sacrifice beat
→ The prophecy twist (75-85%)
→ The payoff: identity-as-weapon, the world's rule beaten
→ The carried magic ending (the world changed, the bond warm)
```

Dense in the bargain scenes, the cost scenes, and the payoff; sparse in the travel and court filler (one world detail per scene). The magic's rules must stay consistent — a broken rule (without a stated cost) is a contract breach the reader will notice.

---

## Ending patterns

- **The carried magic**: the ending shows the world changed (the frost lifted, the court reformed, the door closed) and the bond settled — the magic's rules intact, the couple inside them, warm.
- **The loophole wedding**: the marriage/union performed through the world's own law (the bargain fulfilled, the prophecy completed, the debt paid in the one unasked way) — the world's rule becomes the blessing.
- **The cost shared**: the ending's price is paid by both, together (the shared memory, the divided years) — the sacrifice beat's resolution, no debt unpaid.
- **The threshold scene**: the final image is them at a threshold (the door, the border, the court's gate) choosing each other once more — callback to the opening collision, revalued.
- HE is a hard constraint for this pack's main line: the couple ends together, the world saved or at least held. Bittersweet endings (the price was real, the world still costs) are valid within HE — the bond must survive. If the emotion goal is a sacrificed ending, that's the dark-romance or horror register; decide in Phase 1.

## Genre-specific tuning (deviations from the general de-AI rules)

These read as AI-flavor elsewhere but are selling points here — **don't cut them**:

- **Rules-through-use exposition**: keep the magic surfacing in action and cost; never convert to a lesson. The rule stated once in dialogue, then shown.
- **Contract dialogue**: keep the bargain precision; the loophole payoff is the genre's signature.
- **Bond-ache physicality**: keep the physical intimacy language (the tug, the cold, the shared heartbeat); it's the register's love language — but rotate the image per scene (never the same ache twice in a row).
- **The cost scenes**: keep the concrete payments (memory, years, season); abstract cost ("it cost her dearly") is a genre violation.
- **The identity-as-weapon finale**: keep the protagonist winning through her own specifics; passive rescue by the magic reads as fantasy-dated.
- **World texture one-detail-per-scene**: keep the concrete world details (the lantern, the flour, the north gate); a detail catalogue is padding.

Still avoid the genuine AI-flavor list: "the kind of X that Y", "a wave of magic washed over" (show the cost and the effect), "his eyes were like molten gold" (one concrete wrong/right detail instead), "in that moment", "she felt a surge of power" (show the spell's result), "the air crackled with magic" (one effect, one cost), and "lo!"-register narration — the fantasy voice stays plain-English with world texture, never thees-and-thous unless a specific character genuinely speaks that way.

## Structural formulas (by length)

- **The bond (8-11 sections)**: 1-2 the binding + the world's rule in use → 3-4 the mission + first cost → 5-6 bargain scenes + bond-ache beats, court pressure → 7 the mistaken identity reveal → 8 the forced separation (low point) → 9 the prophecy twist → 10-11 the payoff + carried magic. First person; the fae lead's POV optional as one planted chapter.
- **The portal (8-10 sections)**: the wrong-door/returned-person frame → the world's welcome + the debt that summoned them → the tutorial-as-plot (each section one rule learned through one consequence) → the world-threat → the choice beat (stay/go, mortal/fae) → the payoff + threshold ending.
- **The court intrigue (10-13 sections)**: the masque/marriage-of-law frame → the court's contract game (one move per section) → the queen's scheme as the plot's clock → the bond discovered/strengthened in the margins → the reveal (whose debt, whose prophecy) → the court beaten by its own rules → the loophole wedding. The most political register; keep the romance scenes intimate and the politics concrete.

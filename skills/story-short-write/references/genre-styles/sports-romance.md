# Genre Style Pack: Sports Romance (Short-Form)

> English short-form serialized romance for Wattpad / Kindle short reads (KU) / Inkitt / Galatea. Read `short-craft.md` first; this pack layers the genre-specific voice on top.
> One-line positioning: **rivalry-to-romance on the season's clock — locker-room banter, championship stakes, the trade that separates them, the confession at the final whistle.** The reader wants the competitive tension that is actually foreplay, the public spectacle that forces the private truth, and the couple who realize, mid-season, that the rivalry was a conversation.

---

## Narrative voice

First person, either lead (Wattpad serial convention; the underdog's POV carries most rivals-to-lovers serials), or close third for KU shorts. The voice runs on **the game and the grind**: the narration uses the sport's vocabulary for feelings — the shift, the read, the play that worked, the season as the relationship's clock. Internal monologue alternates between the competitive register (denying, needling, keeping score) and the private register (the bus, the injury, the late-night text). The sport's mechanics appear through consequence, never broadcast filler.

Dual track: the **rival track** (public, competitive, denying — the trash talk, the press, the teammates' commentary) and the **private track** (the shared stall, the injury room, the almost-caught). The two tracks converge in the final game: the public confession is the genre's signature, because the rivalry has always been the couple's only audience.

---

## Opening patterns

A 120-220 word blurb opens with the collision or the stakes in mid-motion + one line naming the rivalry's origin; the body's first section re-shoots the collision with texture.

| Opening type | Example |
|--------------|---------|
| The trade | "The trade sent me to the same team as the man who ended my last season with a hit that was definitely legal and definitely personal. The welcome dinner was a taco truck, and he was holding the hot sauce, and he looked at me like I was a penalty he was planning to take." |
| The paired stall | "They paired us in the same stall, because the coach is either a genius or a sadist. 'Rules,' he said, on day one. 'Don't touch my stuff. Don't talk to me before games. And don't look at me like that.' 'Like what?' I said. He didn't answer. That was day one." |
| The bet | "The bet started at a charity game: first to fifty goals buys the other dinner. It's been a month. I'm winning, he's insufferable, and last night he scored the tying goal and celebrated by looking at me — at me — and I had to tell the whole team I was just mad about the goal." |
| The press | "The reporter asked him about the rivalry on live TV, and he said, 'It's not a rivalry. It's a conversation.' The clip has eight million views. My phone has not stopped. Neither, apparently, has his." |
| The championship | "One game left. The whole season comes down to us, and the whole arena knows the story between us, and the last thing he said before we stepped out was not about hockey. It was: 'If we win this, I'm telling you everything.' We won. He hasn't said a word for an hour, and the trophy is on the bus, and I'm standing in the hallway waiting." |

## Hook motifs and density

The hook is **the score between them**: every section either changes the score (a win, a bet, a goal), raises the public stakes (press, trade, playoffs), or converts one competitive beat into one private beat. End each section on a banter line that lands wrong, an almost-caught, or the season clock moving.

- **The rivalry** (head motif): the score between them — the stolen position, the childhood game, the hit that was "legal and personal." The rivalry is the attraction, never a wall; every relationship beat is also a competitive beat.
- **The bet**: the wager as courtship — the dinner, the jersey, the loser's forfeit; the bet's stakes escalate with the relationship, and its resolution is a kiss or a confession in disguise.
- **The trade / transfer**: the professional move that separates or pairs them — the genre's separation engine; the trade deadline is the countdown, and the "traded back" is a love letter in contract form.
- **The press**: the quote, the clip, the headline — the public register of the private thing; every press scene forces the couple to answer for what the room saw.
- **The locker room**: the forced proximity — the shared stall, the team bus, the playoff hotel; the teammates are the genre's Greek chorus, and their commentary is the reaction shot.
- **The injury**: the care reveal — the hit that goes wrong, the knee, the concussion; the rivalry's standing order ("nobody else gets to hurt him") is the love language.
- **The almost-caught**: one interrupted moment per 2-3 sections (the hand, the kiss, the text), each paid for by a guard going back up — the push-pull of the private track.
- **The championship clock**: the season's countdown — regular season, playoffs, final — the plot's deadline; the confession lands at the final whistle, never before (see rhythm).
- **The public confession**: the genre's signature — the truth said in front of everything (the arena, the cameras, the whole season); the confession is short, factual, and competitive to the last ("your turn").

## Emotional intensity and patterns (intensity 7)

The engine is **competition as courtship**: the banter is the flirtation, the score is the tension, and the vulnerability is earned one private scene at a time. Emotion is named outright in the private register, then landed on a sport-specific object (the jersey, the stall, the puck, the ring).

- The competitive register: needling, short, deniable — the trash talk that is actually flirting; the reader must feel the attraction in the insults, not between them.
- The private register: short, vulnerable bursts — the bus at 2 a.m., the injury room, the text that takes an hour to write. The private register is the genre's intimacy; it runs sparse and lands hard.
- The banter register: the volley — quick lines, comebacks, the crowd (the teammates) counting the hits; one-liners that land are the genre's currency.
- The low point: the public stakes break the private thing — the trade, the press leak, the injury, the loss — and the rivalry's defense ("we're just competitors") becomes the hurt.
- The payoff: the championship + the public confession in the same scene — the win and the truth landing together; the answer is an action (the kiss in front of everything, the hand, the "your turn").

Sample passage (register demonstration — the private track):

> The bus was dark except for the phones, and the team was asleep in the shapes only athletes sleep in — legs over armrests, heads on duffel bags, one guy fully upright with his eyes closed like a man accepting a sentence. I was awake. He was awake. Of course we were awake; we were the only two people on the bus who knew what the other one was thinking, and the bus was full.
> "You're doing the thing," he said, quietly, from two seats over.
> "What thing?"
> "The thing where you pretend you're not watching me think about the game."
> "You think about the game constantly. That's not a tell."
> "I'm not thinking about the game." He said it flat, and the bus hummed, and I did not look at him, because I knew exactly what he was thinking about, and I had been pretending I didn't for three months.
> "You should sleep," I said. "The playoffs start Friday."
> "I know when the playoffs start." He shifted, and I heard the leather of his jacket against the seat. "I'm just wondering if you're going to spend the whole series pretending the bet was about the goals."
> "The bet was about the goals."
> "The bet," he said, "was about who gets to say it first."
> The bus hit a pothole. Somewhere behind us, a teammate grumbled in his sleep. I counted to ten, and I did not turn around, because if I turned around I was going to find out which one of us had been winning the bet, and I was not ready to lose.
> "First to fifty," I said.
> "First to fifty." I heard him settle back. "I'm at forty-nine."
> I had scored that night. I had scored twice. He knew. That was the whole problem with the rivalry: he always knew, and I was starting to think that was the point.

Second sample (the payoff — the final whistle):

> The final whistle went, and the arena became a noise that had no shape, and the bench emptied over the boards like a dam breaking. I found him in the pile — I'd have found him in any pile, that was the other thing the rivalry had taught me — and he was laughing, actually laughing, his helmet off and his face red and his eyes on me, and he said the thing he'd been saving all season:
> "Forty-nine."
> "Fifty," I said. "I scored tonight. That's fifty. You owe me dinner."
> "I'll buy you dinner," he said. "I'll buy you dinner for the rest of your life." And he said it so loud that the guys around us went quiet, and then the whole pile went quiet, and the arena noise poured over the top of our silence like water over a stone.
> "Say it again," I said. "In front of the cameras."
> He looked at the cameras, and then he looked at me, and he smiled the smile he'd been hiding behind the trash talk all season — the one that had never once made it to the press conference.
> "It was never a rivalry," he said, into the microphone of the whole world. "It was a conversation. I've been trying to tell you for three years. I'm telling you now: I'm in love with you, and I've got forty-nine reasons, and I just ran out of excuses."
> The cameras got all of it. The crowd got all of it. The team got all of it, and the video got eight million views by morning, and none of it mattered as much as the two seconds before I kissed him, when the whole arena went quiet the way it does when a body stays down — except this time the body was fine, and the body was mine, and the body was exactly where it wanted to be.
> The season ended with a championship. The bet ended with a dinner. The rivalry ended with a kiss in front of everyone, which was, I told him later, the only way it could have ended — because we'd never once managed to do anything quietly, and I wasn't about to start with the rest of my life.

## Dialogue register and killer lines

Dialogue ratio 45-55% — the highest of the romance packs; the genre breathes through exchanges. The power marker is who scores: the competitive lines land like goals, and the private lines land like hits — short, hard, and only after the setup.

**① The competitive register (trash talk as flirtation)**
- "That's the third time you've checked me tonight. The ref's starting to think you like me."
- "You're only winning because I let you. I'm being generous. It's a character arc."
- "I'd say that was a great save, but I'd have to admit you're good at something."
- "The scoreboard's broken. It keeps showing your face."

**② The private register (short, vulnerable, sparse)**
- "I'm not worried about the game. I'm worried about you." — the line that ends all banter, used once, at the low point.
- "The bus is dark. I can't see your face. That's the only reason I can say this."
- "You're the only person I've ever wanted to lose to."

**③ The team register (the chorus)**
- "For the love of the sport, one of them just has to say it." — the team, in unison, forever.
- "They've been 'hating each other' for three years. The stall assignments are alphabetical. They share a locker room. It's a slow burn, is what it is."

**④ The confession register (flat, factual, public)**
- "It was never a rivalry. It was a conversation."
- "I've got forty-nine reasons, and I just ran out of excuses."
- "If we win this, I'm telling you everything." / "We won."
- "Your turn."

## Rhythm skeleton

```
Blurb (the collision or the stakes + one line naming the rivalry's origin)
→ Section 1: the collision re-shot; the rivalry's origin in one scene
→ The bet/paired-stall setup; the banter register opens; the team chorus starts
→ The private track: one private scene (the bus, the injury), the first almost-caught
→ The press/trade raises the public stakes; a misunderstanding with a real source
→ The low point: the injury or the loss; the rivalry's defense becomes the hurt
→ The championship run: sport beats mirror relationship beats
→ The final game + the public confession; the answer is an action
→ The after: the off-season; the callback to the rivalry's origin, revalued
```

Dense in the banter, the private scenes, and the final game; sparse in the season filler (one sport beat per section, each carrying relationship meaning). The score between them must stay consistent — the rivalry's numbers, bets, and standing order are the story's physics, and the confession lands only after the final whistle, never before.

---

## Ending patterns

- **The final-whistle confession**: the truth said in front of everything, at the game's end; the answer is an action (the kiss, the hand, the "your turn") — public, because the rivalry was always their only audience.
- **The bet settled**: the ending resolves the bet — the dinner, the forfeit, the jersey — and the settlement is the relationship's shape (the standing date, the shared schedule).
- **The traded-back**: the professional reality resolved with the romance — the contract, the trade back, the shared team, the ring bought the week of the trade.
- **The next season**: the off-season epilogue — one scene one season later, the rivalry now a running joke between them, the competitive edge intact (the genre's HE includes the score staying close).
- HE is a hard constraint: the couple ends together, and the professional reality (the contract, the trade, the team) resolves with the romance — the HE ignores the sport's logistics at its peril. The competitive edge survives the ending; a couple who have gone soft are a genre betrayal.

## Genre-specific tuning (deviations from the general de-AI rules)

These read as AI-flavor elsewhere but are selling points here — **don't cut them**:

- **Sport-as-feeling vocabulary**: keep the feelings arriving through the sport's terms (the shift, the read, the play, the score); the register is the genre's intimacy. Never translate the sport beat into therapy-speak.
- **Banter volleys**: keep the quick, competitive exchanges; the volley is the flirtation, and one-liners that land are the genre's currency — cut the long speeches, keep the comebacks.
- **The team chorus**: keep the teammates' commentary; the locker room is the reaction shot and the comic relief — one chorus line per scene, never a full chorus scene.
- **The private track's spareness**: keep the private register short and sparse; two private scenes per story at most, and they land hard because they're rare.
- **The public confession's flatness**: keep the confession short, factual, and competitive to the last; melodrama at the confession reads as staged, and the genre's audience has seen eight million clips.

Still avoid the genuine AI-flavor list: "the kind of X that Y", "a wave of X washed over", "in that moment", "he finally understood", "everything was about to change", "fate had other plans", emotion-by-summary instead of behavior, and the broadcast-filler game scene — every play-by-play beat must carry relationship meaning or it gets cut.

## Platform content-line guardrails

Sports romance is lower-risk than the heat-forward packs, but the platform lines still hold:

- **Adult athletes only**: leads and teammates are adults (18+) — pro-league, semi-pro, or college setting with everyone explicitly aged up; no minor characters in romantic or locker-room-romantic roles.
- **Heat tiers by platform**: Wattpad — tasteful-to-implied; the private-track scenes stay closed-door. Kindle/KU and Inkitt/Galatea mature tiers — explicit permitted where the platform allows; mark the heat tier in the plan. The genre's heat lives in the banter and the private register, not in volume of on-page acts.
- **Trash talk stays below abuse lines**: the competitive register is needling and affectionate — no slurs, no personal-attack escalation that stops being flirtation; the teammates' commentary stays on the right side of the team's actual culture.
- **Injury scenes are plot, not spectacle**: the injury is the care reveal — the concern is the scene; graphic medical detail or the injury as torture-porn is off-register.
- **The rivalry stays a conversation**: the competitive edge never becomes harassment, stalking, or coercion-as-courtship; the "he won't leave her alone" framing is a bright-line breach — the genre's tension is mutual, public, and safe.

## Structural formulas (by length)

- **The bet (8-10 sections)**: 1-2 the collision + the bet's terms → 3-4 the banter volleys + the team chorus; the score escalates → 5 the private track (the bus, the text); the first almost-caught → 6 the press/trade stakes; the misunderstanding with a real source → 7 the low point: the injury or the loss → 8 the championship run → 9 the final game + the public confession → 10 the bet settled + the after. First person; either lead's POV per platform. See Formula 14 in `genre-writing-formulas.md` for the beat table.
- **The paired-stall frame (8-11 sections)**: the forced proximity frame (the shared stall, the shared hotel room) → the rules list as the comedy engine → the private track built in the margins → the trade deadline as the countdown → the almost-caught ×2 → the final game → the confession. The rules list is revalued at the end (the rule broken deliberately is the confession).
- **The press-leak variant (8-10 sections)**: the public clip/quote as the opening → the denial beat ("it was taken out of context" — and it wasn't) → the private truth under public pressure → the leak's real source revealed (a teammate, an ex, a handler) → the final game → the confirmation in front of the same cameras that started it.

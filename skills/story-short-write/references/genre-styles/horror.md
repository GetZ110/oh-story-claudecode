# Genre Style Pack: Horror

> English short-form horror for Wattpad / Inkitt / Galatea / Kindle short reads. Read `short-craft.md` first; this pack layers the genre-specific voice on top. Suspense mechanics (information order, layered hooks, expectation relay) live in `hooks-suspense.md`; this pack covers voice, structure, and pattern.
> One-line positioning: **the familiar turns wrong — a rule that must not be broken, a place that is not empty, a face that is almost right — and the dread is structural, not jump-scare.** The reader wants the creeping certainty that something is wrong, the satisfaction of rules learned too late, and an ending that stays in the room after the story ends.

---

## Narrative voice

First person (the witness, the inheritor, the night-shift worker, the new resident) or close third per platform. The voice runs on **calm observation of wrongness**: the narrator reports the abnormal with flat precision, and the horror lives in the gap between the tone and the content. Fear is written as physical certainty (the hair, the pulse, the temperature) in short single beats — never as emotive interiority.

Two tracks: the **rule track** (the narrator who knows or learns the rules — the inheritor of a house with conditions, the practitioner, the night staff) and the **breaker track** (the one who breaks the rule, usually a friend/family member — their breaking is the engine, and their fate is the lesson). The narrator may break a rule once, late, and pay for it; the breaker track exists so the narrator's single mistake lands harder.

**Genre spine — the rules**: horror runs on explicit, concrete rules with consequences (the door stays closed; don't answer the third knock; the mirror stays covered after midnight; never take the short way home through the back field). The rule is stated once, clearly, by a voice with authority; the reader tracks every subsequent scene against it. The rule's origin (the previous resident, the deed, the local history) is a mid-story reveal.

---

## Opening patterns

A 120-220 word blurb opens with the rule + the first wrong thing; the body's first section re-shoots it.

| Opening type | Example |
|--------------|---------|
| The inherited rule | "The house came with three rules, written in my great-aunt's hand and taped to the pantry door: keep one lamp burning after dark; never open the door to the third knock; and if you hear the rocking chair upstairs, the house is already gone. I followed the rules for six days. My sister visited on the seventh." |
| The night shift | "The diner closed at two. It had always closed at two. Then the bell over the door rang at 2:17, and the man who came in ordered coffee, paid with a bill from a bank that burned down in 1974, and asked if I remembered him. I didn't. He said I would." |
| The wrong place | "The shortcut through the back field saved eleven minutes. Everyone knew it, and everyone knew the rule: if the scarecrow is in the corn, turn around. It rained the day I took it anyway. The scarecrow was in the corn. It was also walking." |
| The returned thing | "I buried the doll in the woods on a Tuesday, with the apology and the salt, exactly like the woman at the antique shop told me. On Thursday it was back on the porch, dry, and the note under its arm said: 'I don't hold grudges. I hold things.'" |
| The almost-right face | "My husband came home from the trip three hours early. He hugged me, kissed my forehead, and asked about my day. Everything was right except one thing, and I couldn't name it for a long time. It was the way he said my name. He'd never said my name that way. He wasn't saying it the way he'd always said it — he was saying it the way I did." |

## Hook motifs and density

The hook is **the rule inching toward being broken**: every section either tightens the rule's pressure (a new wrong thing, a tempting shortcut) or shows the breaker's cost. End sections on an unexplained wrongness with a rule-shaped shadow.

- **The stated rule** (head motif): the rule is explicit, concrete, and repeatable; the reader tracks every scene against it. State it once in full, then echo it in fragments — never re-explain it.
- **The breaker**: a friend/family member who breaks the rule with a rational reason (the shortcut, the debt, the "it's just a superstition") — their rationalization is the reader's dread, because the reader knows.
- **The escalating wrongness**: the same object/place/face gets one new wrong detail per appearance (the scarecrow closer, the doll wet, the name wronger) — the escalation is a progress bar; each appearance must add exactly one wrong thing.
- **The almost-right**: the horror is in small deviations from the familiar (the extra room, the backward signature, the dog that won't look at the hallway) — the uncanny register; never the monster in full view too early.
- **The origin reveal**: the rule's cause arrives at 60-70% (the previous resident, the deed's clause, the local history, the thing the town buried) — human history first, then the thing under it.
- **The narrator's single mistake**: the narrator breaks the rule once, late, for a human reason (saving the breaker, checking the noise, believing the almost-right) — the mistake is the climax's ignition and the ending's cost.
- **The borrowed voice**: horror thrives on the familiar speaking wrong — the phone call from the dead number, the recording with one extra voice, the mother's laugh from the wrong room. One such moment per act, placed at the highest-tension point.
- **The countdown of the rule**: some rules are time-bound (before dawn, before the third knock, before the snow melts) — the clock is the section-level engine.

## Emotional intensity and patterns (intensity 8)

The engine is **dread through accumulation**: each section adds a small wrongness; the reader's fear runs ahead of the narrator's. Fear is written as physical, single-beat, and immediately re-contained by action.

- The physical register: one body detail per beat (the hair standing, the pulse at the throat, the hand gone cold) — one sentence, then the narrator acts. Never stack.
- The reportage register: flat inventory of the wrong ("The clock read 3:07. The clock had read 3:07 when I left the kitchen at 2:40. The kitchen has no clock.").
- The rationalization register: the narrator and the breaker explain the wrong away ("it was the wind," "the neighbors," "I must have left it") — each rationalization is a small dread beat, because the reader knows it's wrong.
- The recognition register: the moment the narrator stops rationalizing — one short, flat line ("I knew then what I had been refusing to know.") — landed on a concrete detail, never a summary.
- The breaker's cost: shown once, briefly, with restraint (the empty chair, the closed door, the note) — the genre's lesson; the reader's imagination does the rest.
- The climax: the rule's breaking converges with the thing's arrival — one scene, full, with the rule's fragments echoing; the narrator survives or doesn't per the ending pattern chosen.
- Gore: restraint is power. The most effective horror beat is often the aftermath (what's left, what's missing, what's now in the house). On-page gore, if any, is brief and functional — never decorative.

Sample passage (register demonstration):

> The third knock came at 2:47 in the morning. I counted the knocks the way my great-aunt's note told me to: one, two, three — and then nothing, because the third knock was the door I was not to open.
> My sister was still out. That was the problem. She'd taken my keys, and her phone was dead, and the only person who'd knock at this hour was her, standing in the cold, waiting to come in.
> The knock came again. Three knocks. Then a voice — her voice, exactly, her rhythm, her impatience: "Open the door, it's freezing out here."
> I stood in the hallway with my hand on the deadbolt. The note was still on the pantry door behind me. I'd read it six days in a row. I knew every word.
> "You're not her," I said, to the door.
> Silence. Then, softer, in my own voice: "Then who am I?"
> I put the chain on, backed away, and stood in the kitchen with one lamp burning until dawn, listening to nothing. The nothing was the loudest thing I'd ever heard. In the morning, my sister's key turned in the lock — the real key, the real voice, the real complaint about the cold. I opened the door. She came in. She asked why the lamp was still burning, and I said I'd forgotten.
> I didn't tell her about the third knock. Some rules are for one person, and the house had chosen me.

Second sample (the breaker's cost — aftermath restraint):

> We found my uncle's boots at the edge of the back field, side by side, still laced. He'd left at midnight with the flashlight and the crowbar, after saying the thing the town always says: "It's just a scarecrow. I'm not afraid of a scarecrow."
> The corn was still. That was the wrong part. In a field that size, there's always wind. The corn was still, and the scarecrow was in it, and the boots were at the edge, and nothing else of him was anywhere we could find.
> The sheriff said the usual things. The neighbor said the usual things. My mother stood at the kitchen window for a long time, looking out at the field the way you look at a debt you know you'll have to pay.
> "The rule," she said, finally, "was never about the scarecrow. The rule was about being sure, at night, that you're the one still walking." She turned from the window. "Go lock the doors. All of them. And don't answer if something knocks in a voice you know."
> I locked them. I've been locking them ever since. The corn has been still for three summers now, and I still can't walk past the back field without counting my own footsteps.

## Dialogue register and killer lines

Dialogue ratio 35-50% (lower than romance genres). Dialogue is an information device: rules, warnings, and denials. The power marker is who gets to state the rule.

**① The authority register (rule-staters: the elder, the practitioner, the note)**
- "Keep the lamp burning. Whatever you hear, whatever you think you see, the lamp is the contract."
- "Don't answer the third knock. Not if it's your mother's voice. Not if it's your own. The third knock is never a request."
- "You can use the well, but you cannot take from it. If you take, you'll be expected to give, and the well keeps better books than the bank."

**② The breaker register (rationalization)**
- "It's just a superstition. The town's full of them."
- "You can't seriously believe a scarecrow—" / "It's in the corn." / "It's always in the corn." / "No. It's in the corn and it's facing the house."
- "I took the shortcut. Nothing happened. See? Nothing. It's fine." (The reader knows it's not fine.)

**③ The wrong-voice register (the almost-right speaking)**
- "You look tired. Come sit with me. I'll tell you a story about this house."
- "We've met before. You don't remember, but we've met. That's alright. I remember."
- "Don't scream. You'll wake the good one."

**④ The recognition register (the narrator's flat line)**
- "I knew then what I had been refusing to know, and the knowing changed the house."
- "The rule wasn't about the door. The rule was about who I'd let in."
- "I left the house with one lamp burning. I've never been able to turn a light off since."

## Rhythm skeleton

```
Blurb (the rule + the first wrong thing)
→ Section 1: the rule stated in full; the world establishes
→ The breaker arrives with a rationalization; first escalation
→ Escalating wrongness (one new detail per section); the breaker's cost shows
→ The origin reveal (60-70%): the rule's cause
→ The narrator's single mistake; the countdown converges
→ The climax: rule fragments echo; the thing arrives
→ The ending (per pattern below); the rule continues or the cost lands
```

Dense in the rule-stating, the escalations, and the climax; sparse in transitions. One wrong detail per escalation — stacking three at once kills the dread's accumulation. The rule must never be re-explained after its first statement.

---

## Ending patterns

- **The carried rule**: the narrator survives by following the rule and inherits it — the last line is the rule passing to the next person (the letter, the note, the deed). Dread continues; the reader's room gets colder.
- **The cost paid**: the narrator breaks the rule once for a human reason and pays — the ending is the aftermath, restrained, what's missing instead of what happened.
- **The re-read ending**: the ending re-arranges the opening (the voice at the start was never who it claimed) — the reader flips back and finds the seams.
- **The ambiguous return**: the narrator escapes; one small detail says the wrongness followed (the lamp, the knock, the name) — used when the Phase 1 emotion goal is creeping dread.
- **The open door**: the thing was never defeated, only delayed; the final scene is the delay ending (the salt line holds, for now). No monologues explaining the monster's rules after the climax — the reader assembles; the narrator only acts.

## Genre-specific tuning (deviations from the general de-AI rules)

These read as AI-flavor elsewhere but are selling points here — **don't cut them**:

- **Flat reportage of the wrong**: keep the calm inventory voice; the tone-content gap is the dread engine.
- **Rule repetition**: the rule's fragments echoing at the climax are ritual, not redundancy — keep the exact wording.
- **One physical fear detail per beat**: keep the single-body-detail beats; they're the genre's fear language. Stacking them reads as AI-flavor, not fear.
- **The almost-right dialogue**: keep the borrowed-voice lines; the uncanny register is the genre.
- **Rationalization beats**: keep the "it was the wind" scenes; the reader's knowing is the dread.
- **Aftermath endings**: keep the restraint; the reader's imagination is the genre's best special effect.

Still avoid the genuine AI-flavor list: "the kind of X that Y", "a chill ran down her spine" (show the single body detail instead), "in that moment", "little did she know", "the air was thick with tension" (one concrete wrongness instead), "it was as if the shadows themselves were alive" (avoid the metaphor sheet; one wrong shadow is stronger), and essay-voice connectives. **Also avoid**: self-invented occult systems with no roots (use the ready-made folklore texture: the third knock, the covered mirror, the cornfield scarecrow, the uncanny double), and endings that explain everything (horror's residue dies under explanation).

## Structural formulas (by length)

- **The inherited rule (8-11 sections)**: 1-2 the rule + the house establishes → 3-5 the breaker + escalating wrongness (one detail per section) → 6-7 the origin reveal → 8 the narrator's single mistake → 9-10 the climax + the carried rule ending. First person; single POV; the breaker is the closest person, and their cost shows restraint.
- **The night-shift / liminal (7-10 sections)**: the workplace/place between places (diner, toll booth, empty store) as the frame — the rule is the shift protocol; the wrongness arrives in customers/objects; the clock (the shift's end) is the engine; the ending is the shift that doesn't end.
- **The almost-right home (8-11 sections)**: the uncanny-double premise (the spouse returned wrong, the house with an extra room, the town where everyone knows your name too well) — the escalating wrongness is the family itself; the reveal is what the family buried; the ending is the recognition. Merges with psychological-thriller.md's mechanics when the wrongness might be the narrator's mind; keep the resolution on the uncanny side for this pack.

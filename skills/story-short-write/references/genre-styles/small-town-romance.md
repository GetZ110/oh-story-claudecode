# Genre Style Pack: Small-Town Romance

> English short-form romance for Wattpad / Tapas / Inkitt / Radish / Kindle short reads. Read `short-craft.md` first; this pack layers the genre-specific voice on top.
> One-line positioning: **a town, a community, and two people who learn that belonging is the real love story — the romance and the found family happen in the same frame.** The reader wants the warmth of being known, the seasonal rhythm of a place, and the grumpy-sunshine / returner-local friction that gives the warmth its shape.

---

## Narrative voice

First person or close third per platform (Wattpad YA is first person; Kindle shorts often close third). The voice runs on **community texture**: the town is a character — its gossip, its seasons, its one diner, its committee of elderly observers — narrated with affection and dry humor. Internal monologue alternates between the outsider register (the returner seeing the town fresh) and the insider register (the local seeing it through someone else's eyes).

Dual track: the **grumpy track** (the closed-off lead: the returned city careerist, the grieving widower, the new sheriff) and the **sunshine track** (the warm lead: the baker, the teacher, the farmer, the festival committee chair). The town's ensemble (the diner owner, the retired mailman, the church committee, the kids) comments, meddles, and witnesses — the ensemble is the genre's chorus and its comic engine.

---

## Opening patterns

A 120-220 word blurb opens with the return/arrival + one town-voice line; the body's first section re-shoots the arrival.

| Opening type | Example |
|--------------|---------|
| The returner | "I came home to sell my grandmother's house and be gone in two weeks. The town had other plans. They'd already signed me up for the Harvest Festival committee, the pie contest, and, apparently, a blind date with the widower who fixes everyone's roofs." |
| The inheritance | "The inheritance was a bakery, a cat, and a standing order at the diner for 'the usual, she knows.' I didn't know. The cat did." |
| The fugitive from the city | "I moved to Maple Hollow to fail quietly. Population 2,104, one diner, and a sheriff who introduced himself by asking if I was running from something. I said no. He said, 'Good. Then you'll stay.'" |
| The forced stay | "My car died at the town line, and the mechanic took the engine apart with the solemnity of a man who intended to keep me for a while. He wasn't wrong. The town does that." |
| The holiday setup | "The Christmas pageant needed a new director. The town council voted, and by the time the motion carried I was already holding the script, the budget, and the grudge of the man who'd lost the vote." |

## Hook motifs and density

The hook is **belonging measured in small acts**: every section either adds a notch of community acceptance (the pie order, the committee seat, the porch light left on) or a notch of intimacy (the shared chore, the almost-moment, the known history). End sections on a small-town event with a question mark (the festival, the storm, the rumor).

- **The town as witness** (head motif): the ensemble knows everything and comments on it — the rumor mill is a plot engine (it spreads the misunderstanding) and a warmth engine (it celebrates the couple).
- **The grumpy-sunshine friction**: the closed-off lead resists; the warm lead keeps showing up with pie and patience. The friction is comedy-first; the crack comes when the grumpy one does one unprompted kind thing.
- **The seasonal clock**: the story is structured on a town event (Harvest Festival, Christmas pageant, county fair, first frost, lake day) — the event is the deadline, the set piece, and the reconciliation stage.
- **The known history**: everyone remembers who they both were (high school, the old grief, the town scandal); the past is community property and gets re-lit as the couple warms up.
- **The shared chore**: fixing the roof, baking the pies, staffing the booth — work scenes are the intimacy scenes; the genre's love happens in ladders and flour.
- **The one-phone-call town**: the town's help arrives unbidden (the porch fixed overnight, the casserole line, the snowplow at 5 a.m.) — the community's care is the genre's biggest fantasy.
- **The departure threat**: the returner's two-week deadline, the new job offer, the city call — the threat of leaving is the third-act clock; the choice beat is staying.
- **The festival payoff**: the town event's climax doubles as the couple's public moment — witnessed, celebrated, and commented on by the ensemble.

## Emotional intensity and patterns (intensity 6)

The engine is **warmth with friction**: low-grade intensity, high-grade attachment. Emotion is named gently and landed on small-town objects (the porch light, the pie, the mended fence).

- The warm register: dry humor, fond exasperation, community shorthand ("the usual, she knows").
- The private register: the grief or wound the grumpy lead carries, revealed in one scene, small and specific (the empty chair, the unopened toolbox, the phone he stopped answering).
- The crack register: the grumpy lead's first unprompted kind act — narrated without commentary, witnessed by the ensemble or the reader alone.
- The thaw: one notch per section from the midpoint — the grumpy lead learns the town's rhythm, names a neighbor, stays for a meal.
- The low point: the departure threat activates (the job offer, the sale, the city call) and the grumpy lead must choose — the town's reaction (the casserole line, the petition, the silent porch) is the emotional climax, and it must be shown, not summarized.
- The choice beat: staying is chosen freely, in public, witnessed — the town's cheer is the romance's confirmation.

Sample passage (register demonstration):

> The roof got fixed overnight. I'd mentioned it once, to the wrong person — the postmaster, who was the town's official dispatcher of information and, apparently, of men with ladders.
> There was a note pinned to the new shingles, written on a napkin from the diner: "Seen worse. — R."
> I drove to the diner to thank him. He was at the counter, coffee in hand, not looking at me.
> "I didn't ask anyone to fix my roof," I said.
> "Nobody asked anyone to do anything." He set the coffee down. "The town votes. You lost."
> "The town voted to fix my roof?"
> "The town voted that you stay." He finally looked at me, and there was a crack in the grump, small as a hairline. "The roof was the excuse. I've got a list of excuses, if you need more."
> "How long is the list?"
> "Long enough for a season," he said. "If the season's long enough."

Second sample (the choice beat — departure threat):

> The city called on a Tuesday. The job was real, the money was real, and the apartment I'd left was still under my name. All I had to do was say yes and the two weeks were over.
> I drove to the diner instead, because that was where the town kept its answers, and he was there, coffee in hand, not looking surprised to see me.
> "The city called," I said.
> "Did it."
> "It's a good job."
> "Is it."
> "Say more than two words." / "The pie's on the house tonight. The house has been saving it."
> I sat down. "I came here to sell a house and leave in two weeks. It's been six months." / "The town votes slow," he said. "It's still counting." He pushed the coffee across the counter. "So am I."
> The door opened behind me, and somebody said my name, and somebody else said they'd saved me a seat at the window. I looked at the coffee, at the window seat, at the man who'd fixed my roof and never once asked me to stay.
> "The city can wait," I said. "The pie can't."

## Dialogue register and killer lines

Dialogue ratio 45-60%. The power marker is town shorthand: locals speak in few words with whole histories loaded; the outsider's longer, more careful sentences mark her as not-yet-from-here — until her sentences get shorter.

**① The town register (shorthand, history-loaded)**
- "You're the one in the Miller house." / "I'm the one renting it." / "Same thing. The house decides."
- "Pie's on the house. The house has opinions."
- "He fixed your roof." / "I didn't ask." / "Nobody asked. He's not good at being asked. He's good at roofs."

**② The grumpy register (short, dry, deflecting)**
- "I'm not unfriendly. I'm efficient."
- "The town's like a bad cold. Gets in everywhere."
- "I don't need help." / "The roof says otherwise." / "The roof and I have an understanding."

**③ The sunshine register (warm, persistent, specific)**
- "I brought pie. It's not for the roof. It's for the man who fixed it and won't say why."
- "The festival needs a judge for the pie contest. You're the only one who's ever told my lemon meringue the truth. I need an honest enemy."
- "You can leave in two weeks. But the porch light's on until you tell me it isn't."

**④ The surrender register (the choice beat)**
- "I came to sell the house. I'm staying because the town fed me, fixed my roof, and learned my coffee order without being asked. That's not a reason to stay. It's a whole stack of them."
- "The list of excuses ran out," he said. "So I'm switching to reasons."

## Rhythm skeleton

```
Blurb (return/arrival + one town-voice line)
→ Section 1: the arrival re-shot; the ensemble establishes the town
→ The friction: grumpy vs. sunshine, rumor engine starts
→ Shared chores + community acts; the crack (first kind act)
→ The known history surfaces; the departure threat arrives (midpoint clock)
→ Warmth escalates toward the town event (one notch per section)
→ The low point: departure threat activates; the town's reaction shown
→ The choice beat (public, witnessed)
→ The festival payoff + HE (settled into the town)
```

Dense in the shared-chore scenes and the festival; sparse in the transitions (town texture one detail per scene). The ensemble gets one line or one act per section — never a full scene of its own unless it's the rumor engine at work.

---

## Ending patterns

- **The festival payoff**: the town event's climax doubles as the couple's public moment — witnessed, celebrated, commented on.
- **The porch-light callback**: the opening image (the house, the porch, the pie) returns, revalued — "the usual, she knows" now includes him.
- **The settled scene**: one season later, the couple is part of a committee/booth/crowd — the ensemble includes them now; the last line is town shorthand.
- **The wedding-at-the-festival variant**: the marriage is folded into the town event (the pageant, the fair), keeping the ending on the community stage. HE is a hard constraint for this pack's romance line: the couple stays, the town keeps them.

## Genre-specific tuning (deviations from the general de-AI rules)

These read as AI-flavor elsewhere but are selling points here — **don't cut them**:

- **Town shorthand dialogue** ("The house decides."): keep; the compression is the genre's texture. Never gloss the shorthand for the reader.
- **Ensemble commentary**: keep the chorus one-liners; they're the warmth and the comic engine.
- **Grumpy deflection**: keep the dry short lines; don't warm the grumpy lead up early — the late thaw is the payoff.
- **Seasonal texture**: keep the specific festival details (the pie recipe, the pageant prop, the parade float); the concrete ritual is the genre's immersion.
- **Community acts**: keep the casserole-line / overnight-help scenes; they are the genre's biggest fantasy and must be shown, not summarized.
- **Warmth without irony**: keep the sincere register; a small-town romance that undercuts its own warmth reads as AI-fear, not voice.

Still avoid the genuine AI-flavor list: "the kind of X that Y", "a wave of nostalgia washed over", "in that moment", "her heart swelled with warmth" (show the casserole line instead), "the air smelled of cinnamon and possibility" (one concrete detail instead), and the ensemble becoming indistinguishable chatterboxes — give each town voice one speech pattern.

---

## Structural formulas (by length)

- **The returner (8-11 sections)**: 1-2 arrival + friction → 3-4 the town's meddling + shared chores → 5 the crack (kind act) + known history → 6-7 warmth toward the event, departure clock runs → 8 low point: the choice → 9-10 festival payoff + HE. First person; the ensemble as chorus.
- **The forced stay / car-dead premise (8-10 sections)**: the mechanical/weather hold → the town's workaround (job, committee, cat) → the grumpy lead's reasons to leave vs. the town's reasons to stay → the departure threat → the choice + HE.
- **The second-chance-in-a-small-town (8-11 sections)**: the estranged pair both live in the town and the town has opinions — merge second-chance.md's estrangement mechanics with this pack's ensemble and event structure; the town is the third party that keeps re-lighting the past.

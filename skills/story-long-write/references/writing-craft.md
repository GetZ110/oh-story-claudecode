## 1. Body details replacing emotion words

### Rule

Key emotion nodes forbid writing the emotion word directly; replace with body states, physical damage, or daily actions; low-intensity transition emotions may be written straight in one line ("he was a little annoyed"), no forced externalization everywhere.

### Replacement operations

Scan the prose for emotion words and replace one by one:

| Banned | Replace with |
|------|--------|
| heartbreak | fingers dig into the palm and he doesn't feel it / she sets the teacup down, blows the foam, doesn't drink |
| sadness | he folded his coat three times and put it back in the deepest shelf of the wardrobe |
| anger / shaking with rage | the veins on the back of her hand stood out one by one |
| fear | his fingers touched the doorknob and pulled back; it took three tries to grip it |
| grievance | she bit her lower lip until a white mark showed; when she let go, it flushed red fast |
| despair | he sat there; ashes fell all over his trouser leg and he never flicked them off |
| dead inside | she flipped the phone face-down on the table and never picked it up again |

The right column only shows direction — copying it is banned: craft each per character, scene, and object; the same writing may appear at most once in the whole book; copying or reusing makes the replacement itself a new template fingerprint.

### Body-part quick reference

Writing: concrete part + concrete action/state, no abstract feelings.

| Part | Sentence template |
|------|----------|
| hand/fingers | "Her hands rested on her knees, fingertips pinching the pleats of her skirt one by one" |
| eyes/pupils | "His pupils contracted, then quickly returned to calm" |
| lips | "Her lips pressed into a line and stayed for a long time" |
| breathing | "She drew a breath as if to speak, then slowly let it out" |
| back/shoulders | "His back stayed perfectly straight — until he was out the door, and it collapsed" |
| scars/bruises | "The cuff slid up an inch, showing the ring of old scar around his wrist" |

## 2. The recurring object system (three-appearance rule)

### Rule

Each narrative unit (short-form piece / long-form volume) designs 1-2 recurring objects. Each object must appear 3 times, with a different meaning each time.

### The three-appearance orchestration

| Appearance | Function | Example |
|----------|------|------|
| 1st appearance (first 1/4) | establish the initial meaning | gold lock = birthday gift from the sister (warmth) |
| 2nd appearance (mid turn) | meaning overturned | gold lock = fake, copper under the gold (hypocrisy) |
| 3rd appearance (ending) | emotional punch | the gold lock thrown into the trash (total rupture) |

### Object design template

Fill the template into setting.md:

```
Object: {name}
1st appearance: Chapter/beat {N} | {scene} | Meaning: {initial}
2nd appearance: Chapter/beat {N} | {scene} | Meaning: {turn}
3rd appearance: Chapter/beat {N} | {scene} | Meaning: {ending}
```

### Object type quick reference

| Type | Trait | Example |
|------|------|------|
| Token | symbol carrying a relationship | gold lock (sister's gift), wooden horse (carved by father) |
| Tool | functional daily object | the scorecard (daughter-in-law), the ledger (debts) |
| Trace | residual body/environment marks | old wrist scar, height marks on the wall |
| Number | concrete amounts/years carrying a relationship | an eighty-thousand-dollar bill, twenty years, eight hundred dollars |

## 4. Numbers/amounts as narrative tools

### Rule

Use concrete numbers instead of vague descriptions. Numbers carry emotional weight; number changes advance the plot.

### Operations

| Scene | Operation | Example |
|------|------|------|
| Build weight | replace "a long time"/"a lot" with concrete amounts/years | "eight years together" not "together for ages" |
| Escalating harm | numbers grow step by step | 800 → 1000 → 80,000 → 1.2 million |
| Contrast punch | extreme inflation then a sudden drop to a tiny value | "My sister owed eighty thousand to put me through school. My brother-in-law transferred me one dollar." |
| Time weight | years carry the emotion | "Twenty years — and she called me Mom for the first time" |

### Number-narrative template

```
Number line:
Beat {N} | {number 1} | Meaning: {initial}
Beat {N} | {number 2} | Meaning: {escalation}
Beat {N} | {number 3} | Meaning: {punch/reversal}
```

## 5. One-motion-one-still rhythm

### Rule

Alternate action and stillness inside each beat. After motion comes stillness; after stillness may come motion.

### Motion and stillness categories

| Type | Content | Example |
|------|------|------|
| Motion | physical impact, violence, conflict bursts | killing, smashing things, slaps, blood-spitting, flipping tables |
| Stillness | daily details, micro-actions, quiet observation | wiping dust, feeding porridge, folding clothes, blowing foam, straightening collars |

### Operations

- Each beat must contain ≥1 "motion" + ≥1 "stillness"
- No 2 consecutive all-motion beats (violence fatigue)
- No 2 consecutive all-stillness beats (rhythm drag)
- The emotional peak (comeuppance/reversal/reveal) uses "motion"
- The emotional low (heart-dead/afterglow/release) uses "stillness"

### Rhythm orchestration example

```
Beat 5 (motion): At Xiao Yan's single order, Xiao Lang took twenty strokes of the cane
Beat 6 (stillness): Xiao Yan crouched, wiping the dust off his son bit by bit, straightening his collar
Beat 7 (motion): Lady Wang lunged over, kneeling and knocking her head on the ground
Beat 8 (stillness): Xiao Yan didn't look at her. He picked up his son and walked away
```

## 6. Opening event density

### Rule

The first 100 words must contain ≥3 events. No background setup; go straight into the event chain.

### Operations

- Delete all opening background introductions ("In the X dynasty…" / "She was a girl who…")
- Sentence 1 is an event, not a description
- Density check: count the independent "things that happened" in the first 100 words

### Event-density examples

```
❌ Low density (1 event / 100 words):
"Shen Zhi was the eldest legitimate daughter of the Shen family, clever since childhood and deeply loved by her father. One day, she received an imperial edict."

✅ High density (4 events / 50 words):
"The day Xiao Yan returned to court, his mother was already dead, his son had been beaten simple by the prince, and he walked into the palace with his blade drawn."
(returned → mother dead → son abused → he attacks: 4 events)
```

Density means how many things happen in one passage — not splitting one thing into one sentence per beat with every sentence broken; the example above links four events with commas in one sentence and is still high density.

### Density check template

After writing the opening, list the first 100 words' events:

```
Event 1: {event}
Event 2: {event}
Event 3: {event}
(If fewer than 3, rewrite the opening)
```

## 7. Beat density diagnosis

### Rule

If a beat feels short after writing, run the diagnosis checklist below. Do not add description just to fill words.

### Diagnosis checklist

Beat feels short? Check in order:

| Check item | Short cause | Remedy |
|----------|------|------|
| All three dimensions of sub-events woven in? | only action, no sensory anchors or body reactions | weave in sensory details and body actions (section 8) |
| Only 1-2 dialogue rounds? | missing dialogue clash | add one power-game dialogue round (section 3) |
| Emotion words present (heartbreak/sadness/anger)? | abstract emotion words used | replace with body details (section 1) |
| Still short after three-dimension weaving? | missing dialogue or recall | add one dialogue round (section 3) or a short recall (section 7 extended rule) |

### How to expand when sub-events are scarce

Writing the outline and sub-events are scarce? Choose in order:

```
IF the main event is conflict/confrontation → add "blockage": the protagonist interrupted or stopped midway
IF the main event involves side characters → add "reaction": a side character's unexpected reaction
IF the main event has spatial movement → add "discovery": a new clue found en route
IF the main event triggers recall → add "flashback": insert a 2-3 sentence short recall
IF the main event is a chain of actions → add "escalation": chain reactions from the action
```

### Task blockers: only when "getting something done would naturally get stuck"

A task blocker is not extra process, not a naturalness prop; it makes the character get stuck for a moment on a concrete thing they're trying to get done, and the block produces a change.

Judgment sentence: the character was going to get this done anyway → this step gets stuck → the character chooses / pays a cost → the block produces information, relationship, cost, choice, or foreshadowing change.

Usable moments:
- Main event is confrontation: the block exposes the opponent's power, rule loopholes, or hidden follow-ups
- Main event is investigation: the block forces out new evidence, new witnesses, or new info gaps
- Main event is relationship advancement: the block forces the character to take a stance, help, refuse, or betray
- Main event is a low-pressure transition: the block is light resistance only, serving relationship, foreshadowing, or life texture — not stealing the show

Delete test: if deleting loses no information, emotion, relationship, cost, or foreshadowing, it is not a useful blocker; do not add process for naturalness or word count.

### Anti-padding rules

Forbidden:
- Adding environment description to fill words ("the sun was bright outside the window")
- Repeating already-expressed emotion to fill words
- Adding character interior-monologue summaries to fill words
- Meaningless character actions to fill words
- Adding process details for naturalness; delete if deletion loses nothing
- Using emotion words instead of body details to fill words (the reverse is correct)

## 8. Scene writing (three-dimension weaving)

### From chapter outline to prose: play the spec as scenes, don't copy the shape

The chapter outline's fields are the spec of "what happens" in this chapter, **not the shape of the prose**. Filling the outline's shape cell by cell turns prose into telegraphic itemized delivery — one beat per line, stiff explanation — this is "copying the outline shape," an AI-flavor source just like copying example shapes.

- The plot-point sequence is a list of "what happens," not a sentence template: don't write one plot point as one sentence or one paragraph in sequence; play each point as action, dialogue, objects, and body reactions the character perceives right now — **act it out, don't announce it**
- The five-part structure (cause/development/turn/climax/ending) is a functional breakdown for the outline author, not five natural paragraphs of prose; prose need not narrate sequentially, nor one paragraph per part
- "Who did what" summary sentences, "A→B→C" arrow chains, and "closes into what state" are planning language; do not transplant them into narration
- Plot points may merge, interleave, and reorder, weaving adjacent points into one continuous scene; dense points expand into slow motion, light points skim (see "dense/light allocation")
- The chapter end lands on the last concrete action, image, line, or suspense; the outline's "ending design / settled state" is planning language — don't write state summaries like "just like that…" / "he finally understood…" into the prose (ending de-elevation in anti-ai-writing.md)

### POV stance: deep limited (grounded immersion)

Before three-dimension weaving, set the narration stance first. Default: lock the POV character's "this-moment perception" — write only what he/she sees, hears, smells, feels bodily, and flashes through the mind right now. This one rule solves the two most stubborn AI flavors at once: weak immersion and the "author present" lecturing/god voice.

- **The camera does not pull back, does not look down, does not enter others' minds**: don't write camera-POV lines like "the whole hall fell into dead silence"; write "she heard her own heartbeat; the breathing around her had gone silent"
- **The reader learns with the character**: what the character doesn't know isn't written; no early spoilers, no background completion; suspense comes from "she doesn't know either" — spoiler sentences like "what she didn't know was" are banned
- **Thoughts are part of action**: inner state presented as "flash + body" — half-sentences, interrupted, emotionally biased subjective judgments; no complete rational monologues
- **Subjective bias replaces objective narration**: the scene is stained by the character's emotion. Whomever she hates is written detestable; when she's panicked, the light wavers. No neutral, everyone-would-see-the-same descriptions. But it must be her biased instant feeling, not objective verdict sentences like "as if pronouncing a long-decided judgment" (that's the author stepping in to conclude; see anti-ai-writing.md Pattern 8)

Deep limited is the root of "naturally de-AI'd": nail the camera inside the character's body and the author has no position to step out and explain, summarize, or arrange (paired with anti-ai-writing.md Pattern 8).

### Style fingerprint: borrow cadence by genre, don't apply a universal voice

Different genres' real style cadences affect the reading feel; effective writing differs. Male-audience/military can borrow legacy web-fiction's continuous-narration inertia; modern daily can write ordinary task advancement; mystery, sci-fi, and historical must not force the legacy voice or first-person mannerisms — first read this book's `setting/style.md`, the benchmark teardown, and the matched chapter summary.

Landing order: first write smoothly per this chapter's emotion and genre style, then handle explanation-summaries, overcompression, and metaphor stacking; don't mechanically lengthen/shorten sentences, don't globally replace with a benchmark book's lexicon.

### Rule

Each sub-event is not one sentence; it carries three dimensions: what happens, what the protagonist notices, how the body responds. All three dimensions weave into the same continuous passage — never written as separate paragraphs per dimension.

### Three-dimension weaving rules

Detailed sub-events ≥100-150 words: the three dimensions carry different information (happening/perception/reaction) woven into one passage, not each written separately. But not every sub-event is written full — transition/travel/info-dump sub-events get 1-2 sentences (see "dense/light allocation"), giving the word budget to emotion nodes.

| Dimension | Content | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| Happening | the thing appears | 1-2 sentences with one concrete detail |
| Perception | what the protagonist notices | ≥1 sensory detail (visual + touch/hearing/smell), focused on one object or one body part |
| Reaction | how the body responds | a concrete body action (hand/eye/breath/posture), may include one very short mental freeze |

Task-blocker writing also weaves three dimensions: happening = the thing won't get done; perception = the concrete evidence — the returned paper, the held object, the stopped action, the other party's half-sentence; reaction = the character changes strategy, pays a cost, asks for help, forces through, concedes, or files it away. Don't explain "why it got stuck" as a manual, and don't tack an action tail at the scene end to explain meaning.

### Anti-pattern: stacked description (absolutely banned)

Writing the three dimensions as three sequential paragraphs is the AI writing tell the reader sees most often — the same action sliced and written three times:

> ❌ **Stacked (wrong)**:
> Mr. Lin lowered his head, pressing the document flat with his left hand, pen in his right, moving it toward the paper.
>
> His hand was shaking.
>
> His hand was shaking from elbow to wrist; the nib paused on the paper, wrote one stroke, paused again; the leftward slash of that "Lin" came out crooked. He paused, pressed the pen down, rewrote; the finished "Lin" had a crooked slash and the pen still wouldn't steady in his hand.

→ One thing written as happening→perception→reaction in three passages; the same action sliced three times

### The correct way: woven

Three dimensions fused into one continuous passage; the reader gets one complete instant:

> ✅ **Woven (correct)**:
> Mr. Lin pressed the document flat with his left hand, pen in his right — the nib veered the moment it touched paper, the tremor running from elbow to wrist as the first stroke dragged sideways. He paused, pressed the pen down, rewrote; the finished "Lin" had a crooked slash and the pen still wouldn't steady in his hand.

→ happening, perception, and reaction appear together in one passage; the reader sees one continuous picture

### Self-check method

After writing a passage, use the "three-finger cover method": cover the happening info, perception info, and reaction info in turn; check each dimension has content. If a dimension is missing, weave the detail back into the same shot — don't add a new paragraph after.

### Paragraph breaking (against one-paragraph-to-the-end)

Three-dimension weaving doesn't mean stuffing a sub-event into one long paragraph. Weaving solves "the same instant split into happening/perception/reaction paragraphs"; paragraph breaking solves "one paragraph too dense for phone reading."

Break paragraphs by shot/information change, not dimension change:

| Trigger | Break |
|---|---|
| A new action starts | new paragraph, letting the reader see the next beat |
| A new object/clue appears | new paragraph, carrying this information alone |
| The character's gaze shifts | new paragraph, a camera cut |
| Direct speech appears | dialogue on its own paragraph |
| A mental freeze/judgment lands | may be a single-sentence paragraph, creating a pause |

Judgment rules:

- One paragraph carries one shot beat / one action unit, or one unbroken reasoning/atmosphere/emotion chain
- Normal narration paragraphs may be short, but sentences inside don't shatter — narration sentences don't get one-period-per-action, sentence-by-sentence breaks (narration defaults to comma-linked mid-length sentences; see anti-ai-writing.md Rule 3); fine description, reasoning judgments, atmosphere pressure, and emotion settling may be longer, provided it's still about the same thing
- Length is diagnostic, not a forced threshold: break when it feels crowded or mixes multiple actions/info/gaze shifts; don't force short breaks when a complete dramatic unit is unfinished
- Normal prose alternates short paragraphs, slightly longer paragraphs, dialogue, and action paragraphs; high-pressure conflict, comeuppance, and reversal passages may be more fragmented and shorter
- No many consecutive same-length paragraphs; don't mechanically fragment one complete reasoning chain

Before output, one natural-rhythm rearrangement pass is mandatory:

1. Scan each paragraph; first ask "does this paragraph only complete one thing / one shot / one reasoning or emotion chain?" Yes → keep; don't break it for word count alone.
2. If the same paragraph contains a new action, new object, new info, new dialogue, a gaze shift, or a scene end, break at those positions.
3. If several consecutive ultra-short paragraphs still belong to the same shot/thing, merge adjacent sentences — avoid shattering into an outline or poetry lines.
4. If a long sentence stumbles in reading or overloads info, first cut modifiers or split the sentence; after splitting, keep the same paragraph's dramatic unit complete.

### Subject & name rhythm (against "subject density")

The protagonist name/character name carries "subject reset" and "emphasis," not sentence-labeling:

- At paragraph heads, scene switches, multi-character scenes, and POV resets, use the protagonist name to establish the subject
- Inside the same action chain/paragraph, prefer "he/she," action continuation, or omitted subjects — let sentences flow
- At key turns, emotion bursts, identity contrasts, or when the reader needs to re-focus on the protagonist, re-name for emphasis
- Reverse signal: many consecutive sentences/paragraphs opening with the same protagonist name with no subject confusion, scene switch, or emphasis need
- No mechanical whole-chapter name-count judging; the final standard is whether it reads with the "every sentence announces a name" stumble

Wrong fixes: chopping one complete reasoning/atmosphere chain into many paragraphs for shortness; dropping subjects so far in multi-character scenes that reference becomes unclear.

Correct fixes: keep one thing's continuity; break at new actions/objects/info/dialogue; name at paragraph heads to establish the subject, pronouns/omission mid-paragraph, re-name at key turns.

### Dense/light allocation (uneven detail)

Don't make every beat the same length and detail — even weighting is the root of the AI voice and the reason "everything's full, nothing stands out." Allocate ink by plot weight:

| Beat type | Ink | Writing |
|---|---|---|
| payoff/comeuppance/reversal/emotion climax | dense (detailed) | perception, action, dialogue clashes filled; slow motion beat by beat |
| transition/travel/info delivery/time skip | light (skim) | 1-2 sentences, even one summary sentence; no three-dimension expansion |
| setup/daily/relationship warming | mid | one or two immersive details written real, the rest skimmed |

Principle: a chapter's detailed beats concentrate on emotion nodes; transitions are firmly compressed. The reader remembers the dense places' imagery; the light places only deliver them fast to the next payoff. Density undulates; rhythm breathes.

### Sub-event connections

Connect sub-events with body actions, not narration transitions. Connection actions ~20 words:

- ❌ "Then I turned the page."
- ✅ "I set the ledger on my knees; my palms had gone slick."

### Perception material library

| Scene type | Usable perception details |
|---|---|
| reading/browsing | ink depth, paper feel, ink bleeding, curled page corners |
| dialogue scenes | the other's expression changes, tone pauses, the silence in the air |
| recall scenes | a detail in the memory suddenly sharpening (smell/sound/touch) |
| indoor scenes | light changes, object positions, temperature |
| movement scenes | footsteps, ground feel, wind direction |

### The three dimensions' boundaries

- ✓ Perception is a detail the protagonist actively notices (has an attention focus)
- ✓ Reaction is a concrete body action (hand, eye, breath, posture)
- ✗ Perception cannot be decorative scene description ("sunlight fell on the windowsill")
- ✗ Reaction cannot be emotion words ("his heart felt like a knife")
- ✗ The three dimensions cannot repeat the same information

## 9. Punctuation tone spectrum (punctuation follows tone)

### Rule

Punctuation carries tone, power relationships, and emotion beats; it is not decoration. When writing prose, first judge "what this sentence does in the scene," then choose periods, commas, question marks, exclamation marks, colons, or line breaks/action beats; no more ellipses and dashes for pauses.

### Punctuation spectrum

| Tone / function | Punctuation | Operations | Taboos |
|---|---|---|---|
| pressure / calm / restraint | periods, commas, colons, short sentences | pressurize with unruffled statements; land judgments with colons; explain little | no random `!` for "variety" |
| accusation / testing / rhetorical | question marks, short follow-ups, action pauses | key questions get `?`; follow-ups may be standalone short lines | no 5 consecutive all-`?` lines |
| surprise / burst / comeuppance | a few exclamation marks, single-sentence paragraphs | only 1 `!` at the emotion peak; short sentences carry the burst around it | no `!!!`, no whole-paragraph shouting |
| hesitation / unfinished / guilty | commas, periods, short sentences, swallowing/stopping actions | pause with actions or sentence-length change; new short line when needed | no `……` for pauses |
| interrupted / dragged-out | action interruption, line breaks, short sentences, unfinished actions | cut with actions like "he raised a hand to stop her words." | no `——`/`—`/`--` in prose or dialogue |
| info reveal / judgment landing | colons, semicolons, short sentences | "There was one piece of evidence: the lock had been thrown from inside." colons make the landing | no essay-style long semicolon chains |

### Execution steps

1. Read the scene's target emotion and character voice; label every key dialogue beat with a tone class (pressure/testing/burst/hesitation/interrupted).
2. Polish pass: scan sentence endings — if a whole passage has only periods, check whether accusations, hesitations, and bursts got flattened; if the screen is full of `?`/`!`, cut down to the functional positions only.
3. In prose (including dialogue), functional English ellipses and em dashes are allowed for hesitation, interruption, or a deliberate aside. Remove repeated pause padding, `--`, and em-dash clusters. Zhihu Yan's `「」` remains a legacy platform-specific quote style; it does not affect the legal use of question marks and exclamation marks.

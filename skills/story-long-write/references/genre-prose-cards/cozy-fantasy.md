# Cozy Fantasy Prose Card

## Prose directive
Warmth is the product; low stakes mean high attention. The stakes are relational and economic, never apocalyptic — a broken promise, a bad harvest, a rude visitor, an unpaid debt, a closed road. The inn, bookshop, or café is a character with routines the reader learns to expect. Magic is domestic (the bread that rises to the baker's mood, the door that knows the regulars, the kettle that whistles a warning) and costs nothing terrible. The protagonist's competence is the quiet engine: they notice, they fix, they include. The prose is unhurried, sensory, and kind without being saccharine; humor lands from gentle absurdities, and the personal wound heals in the background, one quiet scene at a time.

## Opening hooks
- Cut in at the threshold moment: the new innkeeper arriving, the shop inherited, the first customer, the roof leaking at the worst time.
- First scene establishes the place's routines and the small problem the chapter will solve; the place is introduced through work, not description.
- The protagonist's wound is shown as a habit, not a tragedy — the letter they don't open, the room they don't enter, the name they no longer say.

## Conflict engine
Small problems + community tension + the protagonist's personal wound healing in the background. Conflicts must be resolvable by conversation, labor, or a modest magic; the reader's pleasure is watching competence and kindness applied in the right order. Obstacles have understandable reasons (the merchant's fear, the neighbor's grief, the mayor's pride) — never pure evil.

## Payoff & emotion release
Release comes from the customer returning with a gift, the harvest brought in, the found family at one table, the small magic working exactly right, and the wound touched gently — a letter read, a memory named, a loss acknowledged in a sentence. The release is quiet: a sigh, a full plate, a lit lamp, the regular who finally stays for dinner.

## Dialogue & voice
Warm, plain, character-voiced; teasing is affection, advice is offered and never imposed. Close third with gentle humor; the narration notices small kindnesses and records them without comment. The community speaks in overlapping voices with distinct habits; the protagonist's lines are practical and dry.

## Chapter-end hooks
Use a new customer with a story, a letter arriving, a seasonal deadline (the fair, the winter store, the festival), a small mystery (the locked door, the stranger's lie), or the wound touched again. The hook points at the next small problem with a human face — never at an impending catastrophe.

## Scene granularity
The texture lands on the counter, the kitchen, the garden, the market, the festival, and the hearth; food, weather, fabric, and wood carry the sensory load; the regulars' habits (the chair, the order, the argument about the mayor's cat) build the community; the magic appears in domestic forms (the self-sweeping hearth, the bread, the door).

## Prose landing points
Open on the threshold or a routine; conflict lands on a small practical problem with a human edge; endings land on a shared table or a lit lamp — the world slightly better, one person slightly less alone.

## Early/mid/late play
- Early: establish the place, the routines, the regulars, and the wound in the first chapters.
- Mid: deepen the community; one bigger seasonal problem (the fair, the flood, the festival, the road closure); the found family coheres; the wound is touched twice, gently.
- Late: the season's grand small event; the wound resolved in a quiet scene; the place is home, and one new door is left open for the sequel.

## Pacing density
One small problem solved per chapter, with a cost and a kindness; one community relationship notch per chapter; the seasonal arc carries the mid-book escalation. No consecutive chapters without a solved problem and a warmed relationship.

## This-chapter tradeoffs
The small problem is the plot; the character's wound is the subtext; both must move one visible step. Never raise the stakes beyond the village scale; the chapter's villain, if any, is a misunderstanding or a hard season, not a person to defeat.

## Drift bans
No world-ending stakes; no grimdark turns (the magic never costs a child or a year of life); no villain who is pure evil; no manicured perfection (the place must have one small flaw the community accepts or fixes together); no saccharine (warmth must be earned by labor and specificity — a shared meal is a resolution only if we saw it cooked); no cozy-mystery bait — if a body appears, the genre has changed.

## Sample passages

> 1. The inn had been closed for nine years when Mara opened the door, and the dust had made itself at home. She stood in the front room with the key her aunt had mailed from somewhere warmer and counted the broken chairs: three. The hearth was clean. That was the first thing she noticed, because a hearth that stayed clean for nine years meant someone had been coming in. She built the fire anyway, and when it caught, she heard the floorboards creak in the kitchen, in the pattern of someone settling in to listen.

> 2. The bread rose faster when the baker was happy. Everyone in Thistleknow knew this. What they argued about was whether the bread rose slower when she was sad, or simply did not rise at all, and the argument was a standing one, like the debate about the mayor's cat. "It's a sympathy thing," the blacksmith said. "Bread is a sympathy thing," the baker said, "and so is a roof." She pointed at the patch he had put on her kitchen ceiling the week before. "You fixed mine. I'll raise yours." The blacksmith looked at the loaf she slid across the counter. It was a good loaf, warm and tall. He took it home, and the mayor's cat followed him, which everyone agreed settled nothing.

> 3. The festival needed a third judge for the pie contest, and the town chose the new innkeeper, because new people were believed to have no favorites, which was almost true. Mara tasted nine pies and lied about none of them, because that was her way, and the pie that won was the one nobody had expected — a lavender-and-honey pie from the girl who had been too shy to enter last year. "The crust is patient," Mara said, when the girl asked how she had known. "A patient crust means the hands were calm. The hands are the person." The girl looked at her like she had been handed something she had been waiting for all her life. The inn had a regular after that, and the inn had a pie, and both of them got better with practice.

> 4. The letter was from the harbor, and it was nine years old. Mara read it by the hearth fire, in the chair the creaking floorboards had led her to, and learned that her aunt had not abandoned the inn: the roof had needed a year, the well another, and the hearth — the hearth had kept itself clean because her aunt had never stopped coming back, even after she had stopped telling anyone where she went. The letter ended with the key and a sentence Mara read three times: "The door is the easy part. The welcome is the work." She folded the letter into the ledger, served the lavender-and-honey pie to the girl who had become her best customer, and wrote the date at the top of a fresh page, because the inn had a new keeper now, and the welcome was going to be work worth doing.

# Romantasy Prose Card

## Prose directive
Romance is the spine; the epic fantasy world exists to test the couple. The magic, the court, the dragons, and the war are relationship technology — the bond is the plot, the court is the obstacle course, the dragon is the witness. Write in dual POV (both leads competent, both with secrets); the worldbuilding surfaces through the couple's scenes, never as lecture. Spice-forward but plot-driven: an intimacy scene must advance trust, reveal a secret, or change the power balance, or it doesn't earn its page. The voice is lush but grounded — fantasy vocabulary without stiltedness; banter is the love language, subtext carries the court talk, and the bond's physical language (the thread, the ache, the burn) is the genre's intimacy register.

## Opening hooks
- Cut in at the bond's first sign or the meet-catastrophe: the treaty marriage, the enemy prince in her tower, the dragon claiming its rider, the binding spell cast mid-ceremony.
- First chapter establishes the world's rule and the romance's obstacle in one scene — the war that makes the union a weapon, the court that forbids the match, the curse that punishes attachment.
- Fated pairings still need earned chemistry: a shared scar, a shared goal, or a common enemy. The bond is a pull, never a shortcut past the work.

## Conflict engine
Romance + world threat as one engine: the bond cannot be honored because of the war, the court, or the curse; the world's deadline forces them to trust each other; the court politics test the trust in public. Chapter-level conflict is one relationship-state test per beat — the misunderstanding with a real source (a forged letter, an intercepted message, a political lie), the public humiliation that demands a defense, the mission that forces a choice between the bond and the crown.

## Payoff & emotion release
Release comes from trust earned in public (the champion claiming the heir in front of the entire court), the bond paying off (the spell that only works because they are bound, the second clause no one tested), and the choice beat (the lead choosing the other over power, at the moment power is on the table). The reader wants the slow burn, the banter duels, the spice scenes that move the relationship state, and the public claim that makes the private vow official.

## Dialogue & voice
Dual POV banter, courtship duels, and subtext-heavy court talk. Both leads are competent and say what they mean sideways. Court speech is precise and loophole-ready; the couple's private speech is plain and warmer — the contrast is the intimacy. Never let a character lecture the magic system back to the reader.

## Chapter-end hooks
Use the bond's physical reaction (the pull, the burn, the shared pain), a court betrayal, a dragon or creature discovery, the other lead's secret surfacing, an intimacy scene cut at the door, or the war's deadline moving. The hook points at the next trust test with a world cost.

## Scene granularity
The texture lands on courts, dragon towers, war councils, libraries, battlefields, marriage treaties, courtship rituals, and the bond's physical marks (the thread, the glow, the scar that aches in the other's pain). Court scenes carry seating, gifts, and rumors; the couple's scenes carry small private objects (a borrowed knife, a kept letter, a shared fire) that the world keeps threatening.

## Prose landing points
Open on the bond or the meet-catastrophe; conflict lands on the trust test and the world's deadline; endings land on the public claim or the private vow — with the next world threat already visible.

## Early/mid/late play
- Early: establish the bond and the world's rule in the first chapters; first trust tests and courtship duels; the couple's competence proven in the world's terms.
- Mid: the couple tests the bond against the world (missions, courts, war); one big misunderstanding with a real source; spice escalates as the relationship state advances.
- Late: the world's final threat forces the public choice; the bond becomes the weapon; the ending settles the couple and opens the series hook (new threat, new generation, new bond).

## Pacing density
One relationship-state change per chapter (closer, further, tested, claimed) plus one world plot beat per 2-3 chapters. A spice scene every 3-5 chapters that advances the relationship or reveals character. No consecutive chapters of pure court filler or pure private pining.

## This-chapter tradeoffs
Choose the romance beat or the plot beat as the chapter's primary; the other serves it. One intimacy scene per 3-5 chapters maximum, and each must carry one new piece of information or one relationship shift. Keep the magic's rule statements short; the reaction long.

## Drift bans
No miscommunication-only plots (misunderstandings need a real source and a real cost); no plot-stopping spice (heat that exists only for heat); no fated bond doing the work the characters should do (they still choose each other, scene by scene); no insta-love without a scene proving the ache; no worldbuilding lectures; no cardboard antagonist — the court, the rival, the villain all act on understandable interests.

## Sample passages

> 1. The binding spell took hold at the treaty signing, between the second glass of wine and the third. Seren felt it first in her ribs, like a hand placed on a wound that had not finished closing. Across the table, Prince Cassian went still. He had signed her kingdom's surrender an hour ago. Now he was bound to her by the old rite, and neither of them had known the rite was still legal. "This changes nothing," she said. The bond pulled at her chest, gently, like a question. "It changes the succession," he said. "And the war." She looked at the man who had burned her father's banner and thought about how young he looked when he was afraid.

> 2. He found her in the library at midnight, and she did not ask how he had gotten past her guards, because the bond always knew where she was, and she had stopped pretending that was a flaw in the architecture. "The council will announce the wedding at dawn," he said. "I came to tell you first, so you could hate me properly." "I don't hate you." The words came out smaller than she had planned. "That's the problem, isn't it," he said, and then he was close enough that she could smell the rain on him, and the bond hummed between them like a struck wire, and she thought about how wars end — badly, for the people who wait.

> 3. The queen's spymaster presented her evidence at the noon council: a love letter in Seren's hand, promising the border fort to the northern duke. Seren watched Cassian read it. She had written no such letter, and everyone in the room knew someone had forged her seal. "Well?" the queen said. Cassian set the letter down. "The fort is loyal to me. The duke is loyal to the queen. If my wife had truly written this, she would have sent it by raven, not by courier." He looked at Seren, and the bond went quiet and warm, like an animal that had decided the danger was over. "I know her hand," he said. "This is not her hand." The spymaster's smile slipped, and Seren understood, in the same breath, that she had just been chosen in front of the entire court, and that the choice had cost him something she did not yet know the price of.

> 4. The northern army came over the ridge at dusk, and Seren stood on the wall with her bow, her crown, and her husband, who had spent the morning arguing with her about which of them would die first. "If we lose," she said, "I want it known that I married you for the border." He laughed, and the bond burned bright in her chest, and she realized the spell that bound them had a second clause no one had ever tested: it worked both ways. "Then let's make sure the border holds," he said, and he drew the sword that had burned her father's banner, and raised it, and the army below answered with a roar that carried three kingdoms. The war was not over. But something in her had finally chosen its side, and the bond hummed like a war horn being tuned.

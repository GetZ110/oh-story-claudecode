# LitRPG / System Apocalypse Prose Card

## Prose directive
LitRPG runs on visible numbers and their human cost. The system panel is a character: it states, it rewards, it punishes — and the protagonist must react in the same paragraph, or the panel becomes wallpaper. Write the stats into scenes (a level-up that changes how a door opens, a debuff that makes the stairs longer), never as floating text the story ignores. The prose should alternate between brisk mechanical clarity (what the system says) and warm concrete texture (what it costs the person). The genre voice lives in the gap between the numbers and the body.

## Opening hooks
- Cut in at the moment the system arrives: a status window over the dinner table, the apocalypse countdown, the tutorial spawning in a workplace.
- First scene establishes the rule of the game — what the system wants and what it takes.
- The protagonist's build is a choice, not a gift; their first decisions define the run.

## Conflict engine
System rules + resource pressure + the world changing around an ordinary life. Conflict runs on the cost of progression: what the protagonist spends to level, and who is left behind.

## Payoff & emotion release
Release comes from build synergies paying off, dungeon clears, loot that changes the situation, and the system being outplayed — a condition satisfied sideways, a quest reward double-dipped. The reader must feel the build click: the skill that was useless for forty chapters finally becoming the key.

## Dialogue & voice
System text is cold, terse, and exact — brackets, no wasted words. People speak warm and colloquial around it; the contrast is the genre's signature. Never let a character lecture the system's rules back to the reader.

## Chapter-end hooks
Use a new quest, a threshold reached, a countdown, an unknown monster's spawn, a stat point unspent and argued over, or the system's quiet announcement of a rule change. The hook points at the next mechanical problem with a human face.

## Scene granularity
The texture lands on status windows, quest logs, loot drops, party comps, skill trees, safe zones, and the ordinary objects that gain game properties (a broom that can now crit). The realism comes from the mundane colliding with the mechanical.

## Prose landing points
Open on the system's arrival; conflict lands on resource and build costs; endings land on a visible reward claimed or a rule bent — with the next requirement already visible.

## Early/mid/late play
- Early: establish the system's rule and the protagonist's build; every level shows in the world.
- Mid: escalate dungeon tiers and build synergies; costs compound; the party grows and costs trust.
- Late: bend the system's own rules; the ending settles the build against the world's final exam.

## Pacing density
One system interaction per chapter minimum — a panel, a quest, a reward — each with a visible consequence in the scene. No chapter of pure grinding with no human cost.

## This-chapter tradeoffs
One stat or skill decision per chapter, argued in human terms. Keep the panel short; the reaction long.

## Drift bans
No status-window spam; no quests that exist only to grant rewards; no numbers without consequences; no protagonist who never makes a bad choice. The system must be beatable by cleverness, not by favoritism.

## Sample passages

> 1. The notification arrived during lunch, pasted across her vision like a subtitle on a movie she hadn't turned on.

> [SYSTEM ONLINE]
> [WORLD EVENT: FLOOR ONE UNLOCKED IN 00:59:59]
> [ALL HUMANS: LEVEL 1, CLASS UNDECIDED]

> Maya put down her sandwich and looked around the food court. Nobody else had paused. The man at the next table was still arguing about the ketchup. She was the only one who could see it — or the only one who'd stopped eating. She had fifty-nine minutes to decide what to be. She thought about the ketchup argument, and she chose wrong on purpose, because the tutorial always favored the reckless, and she'd spent ten years of her life proving she wasn't that.

> 2. The skill had been useless for forty-three chapters of her life: [Dowsing, Level 3], a joke skill from a starter pack that let her find water. In the sixth-floor dungeon, the boss's weakness was a dry riverbed running under the arena, and the party had spent two hours failing to drain it. Maya put her palm on the stone. The system prompt appeared, patient as a toll booth. [DOWSING: TARGET FOUND — SUBTERRANEAN SOURCE, 6 METERS BELOW]. The party leader stared at her. "That's the worst skill in the game." "It's the only one," she said, "that reads the map underneath the map."

> 3. The countdown hit zero and the safe zone went dark. [DUNGEON: MIRROR MAZE — REWARD: ONE CLEAR + 50,000 EXP] [WARNING: ENEMY COUNTS UNKNOWN] [WARNING: THE MAZE LEARNS]. The party checked their gear, their potions, their exits. The new guy checked the walls with his hand. "It's learning," he said. "So we stop fighting the maze." "What do we fight instead?" "The lesson." The walls rippled — and for the first time that run, the maze looked confused.

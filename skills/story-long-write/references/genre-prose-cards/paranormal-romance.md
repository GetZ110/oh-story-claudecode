# Paranormal Romance Prose Card

## Prose directive
Paranormal romance anchors the supernatural in the body: the heat, the scent, the pull that has a biological explanation she can't argue with. The monster is romantic precisely because he is dangerous in a way that is *known*. Keep one foot in the ordinary world — bills, diners, a job to keep — so the magic feels like intrusion, not wallpaper. The prose should be sensory-first; the genre voice lives in texture (growls, cold hands, fur, sparks) rather than abstract longing.

## Opening hooks
- Cut in at the moment the ordinary life breaks: a full moon, a stranger who smells like smoke and pine, a wound that heals itself.
- First scene establishes the cost of the supernatural — what she gives up by noticing.
- The heroine is a skeptic or a survivor, not a vessel; her ordinary knowledge is the counterweight.

## Conflict engine
Biological pull + species politics + a world that punishes hybrids and outsiders. The romance conflict runs on what the bond costs her humanity, her family, or her freedom.

## Payoff & emotion release
Payoffs come from the mate bond proving itself, the pack/family being forced to accept her, the hunt turning protective, and the heroine's ordinary skills saving the supernatural. Release on physical proof: a wound healed, a door held, a growl that means "mine" said in front of witnesses.

## Dialogue & voice
The supernatural lead speaks in short, old sentences — centuries of language worn smooth. The heroine speaks contemporary and blunt; her plainness is the charm. Banter between them carries more intimacy than confession.

## Chapter-end hooks
Use a rival pack's challenge, a secret from the old world surfacing, a family discovery of what she's become, the bond worsening, or the hunt arriving at the door. The hook escalates the supernatural stakes while pulling the relationship.

## Scene granularity
The texture lands on moon cycles, pack rules, diner shifts, claw marks, silver, blood-scented air, and the ordinary objects she drags into the supernatural (a first-aid kit that can't help, a phone with no signal). The genre's realism comes from the mundane colliding with the mythic.

## Prose landing points
Open on the intrusion of the supernatural; conflict lands on pack/species politics and bond costs; endings land on a visible supernatural act performed for her — or a human act he performs to stay with her.

## Early/mid/late play
- Early: establish the pull as a problem with a price tag.
- Mid: alternate species politics with private intimacy; every secret revealed raises a new one.
- Late: force the two worlds to meet in public; the ending settles the bond as chosen, not fated.

## Pacing density
One supernatural display or species-politics beat per 2-3 chapters; intimacy scenes between them. No chapter without a sensory anchor.

## This-chapter tradeoffs
Pick one supernatural element to pay for one human one. Keep the heroine's world (job, family, bills) alive in the margins.

## Drift bans
No magic solving relationship problems; no mate bond as a consent shortcut; no werewolf as a generic tall-dark-and-handsome with fur; no lore dumps that stop the scene. The supernatural must cost something.

## Sample passages

> 1. The man at the counter ordered a black coffee and paid with cash that smelled like a campfire. He was too tall for the diner, too still for a Tuesday morning, and when he looked at her, Maya's skin remembered something her brain had never learned. Her hand shook pouring the coffee. "You're new," she said. "Not exactly," he said. His voice was low and old, like a door closing on something heavy. "But you are. You're the first one in a hundred years who's smelled like that." "Like what?" He didn't answer. He left the coffee, untouched, and the bell over the door rang like a warning.

> 2. The bite healed in six hours, leaving a scar that spelled his name in a language she couldn't read. The pack lawyer had a form for it. Of course he did. "Sign," he said, "and the pack covers your rent, your tuition, and your silence." "And if I don't sign?" "Then you remember what you saw." "I don't want your money," she said. "I want to know why your alpha looked at me like I was a debt." The lawyer's pen hovered. "Because you are," he said. "You just don't know what for yet."

> 3. The full moon rose and her pulse doubled before the first howl. She'd locked the door, piled the chairs, and thrown salt she didn't believe in across the windowsill. Then the howling stopped — and a knock came, polite, three times. "Maya." His voice, through the wood, patient as a river. "You don't have to open it. I just need you to know I'm out here so you can stop being afraid of the rest." She pressed her forehead to the door. "And what about being afraid of you?" A long silence. "That," he said, "you should be. But not tonight."

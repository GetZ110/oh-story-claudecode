# Isekai Prose Card

## Prose directive
The other world is a system to be learned and beaten. The protagonist arrives with modern knowledge, a cheat, or a past life — and the story is the application: the world's rules, factions, and economies respond to the leverage. Two registers alternate: the mundane texture of the new world (food, money, hierarchy, weather) and the escalating application of the cheat. The protagonist is competent but not infallible; the world pushes back with its own logic. Male-lead default: agency, progression, clear goals, wry inner voice. The villainess/otome subline inverts the register — female lead, social capital instead of combat power, the game's script as the antagonist — and deserves its own note in any cross-genre read.

## Opening hooks
- Cut in at the moment of arrival: the death rendered fast, the reincarnation compact, the tutorial, the summoning circle, the clerk with the paperwork.
- First chapters establish the world's rule quickly (the game panel, the class, the social system) and the protagonist's first unfair advantage — with its cost or limit visible.
- The cheat must have a price early: the debt, the weakened body, the faction that starts hunting the anomaly.

## Conflict engine
The world's rules + faction competition + the protagonist's cheat with limits. Conflict is progression-oriented: enemies, nobles, guilds, dungeons, monsters, and rivals who notice the anomaly. The engine is the loop: problem → leverage → escalation → bigger problem. Setbacks arrive every 5-8 chapters to keep the world honest; the cheat escalates and its cost grows with it.

## Payoff & emotion release
Release comes from level-ups that visibly change status, the cheat's clever application (chemistry, economics, tactics solving what swords cannot), public recognition, faction rewards, the rival's frustration, and the accumulation of resources and allies. The payoff is forward-looking — it ends on the next requirement, the next door, the next faction's interest.

## Dialogue & voice
Brisk, functional, a little wry. The protagonist's modern voice contrasts with the world's formality — the gap is the humor. System and world text is cold, terse, and exact. Supporting cast speak in faction accents (nobles formal, guild grunts plain, merchants precise). Inner monologue carries planning, not angst; the protagonist narrates problems the way an engineer narrates a broken machine.

## Chapter-end hooks
Use a new quest or level, a dungeon door, a faction move, a rival's scheme surfacing, the cheat's next unlock, a system notification, or a countdown. The hook points at the next mechanical problem with a human or faction face.

## Scene granularity
The texture lands on guild halls, status windows, dungeon corridors, market stalls, noble estates, war councils, item shops, and the protagonist's ledger of knowledge applied — the irrigation system, the preserved grain, the rust-proofed iron, the negotiation that reads like a contract. Realism comes from the mundane colliding with the mechanical.

## Prose landing points
Open on the arrival and the world's rule; conflict lands on the leverage applied and the pushback; endings land on a visible gain claimed and the next requirement already visible.

## Early/mid/late play
- Early: arrival, the world's rule in one chapter, the cheat with its cost, the first faction hook; every gain shows in the world.
- Mid: faction arcs (guild, nobles, kingdom); the cheat's modern knowledge gets world-specific adaptation; rivals consolidate; setbacks compound; allies accumulate and cost trust.
- Late: the faction war or final exam; the cheat's true cost revealed; the public payoff; the ending settles the protagonist's place and opens the next arc.

## Pacing density
One progression event per chapter (level, quest, scheme, gain), each with a visible consequence in the scene. The cheat's escalation every 3-5 chapters; a setback every 5-8 chapters. No chapter of pure grinding with no human cost.

## This-chapter tradeoffs
One application of the cheat per chapter, with one pushback. Keep the system or game text short; the reaction long. Every modern-knowledge fix needs one world-specific adaptation or it reads as magic.

## Drift bans
No cheat without a cost; no protagonist who never loses; no world that exists only to be conquered (factions must have their own goals); no modern knowledge solving everything instantly; no harem-by-default (relationships must be earned or absent); no system text spam; no protagonist who never makes a bad choice.

## Sample passages

> 1. The truck was the last thing he remembered, which was unfortunate, because the first thing he heard in the new world was a clerk telling him he had died owing money. "I'm sorry," the clerk said, not sounding sorry, "but your soul arrived with negative starting funds. The reincarnation fee was deducted." "I didn't sign up for a fee." "Everyone signs up," the clerk said. "It's in the agreement you didn't read." Jae looked at the window showing his new body: a fourteen-year-old boy in a village that apparently rented him out for harvest work. He had one hundred years of modern knowledge, a body with a bad knee, and a debt. "Fine," he said. "Show me the agreement." This is why, three weeks later, the village had a better irrigation system and the reincarnation office had a complaint department with one very tired clerk.

> 2. The skill window had three entries when Kei checked it at the academy gate: [Appraisal — useless], [Taste of Home — lets you recall any meal from your past life in perfect sensory detail], and [Patient Hands]. The class selection panel offered the standard builds — swordsman, mage, archer — and the panel in the corner, the one nobody picked, offered [Artisan]. Kei looked at the tuition fee, at the scholarship notice, and at the line of noble students with enchanted blades. "I'll take Artisan," he said. The panel blinked, as if unsure it had heard right. Two years later, when the kingdom's army collapsed at the border because their supply lines rotted in the rain, the crown council sent for the academy's one student who could cure leather, preserve grain, and explain why the general's iron rusted in a week. The panel had stopped blinking. It had started paying attention.

> 3. The villainess's plan was working too well, and that was the problem. Elia had been reincarnated into the otome game as the girl who gets exiled at the end, so she had done what any sensible person would do: she had made herself too useful to exile. The crown prince's tea — cured. The duke's ledgers — balanced. The queen's migraine — gone, by way of a plant the game designers had labeled "weeds." The game's script had begun to stutter around her. The heroine had been waiting for her villainous scene for four chapters, and Elia kept bringing her pastries. "You're supposed to be my rival," the heroine said, one day, suspiciously, over the third pastry. "I'm trying," Elia said. "I'm just not good at losing." The game's fate system, which had never encountered a player who simply refused to be a plot device, rolled the ending screen early. It was, for the first time in its existence, an ending nobody had written.

> 4. The dungeon was on floor forty, and the party had come prepared for the horror they had read about: the grinder, the maw, the fall. They had not come prepared for the man standing at the entrance with a clipboard. "Welcome to floor forty," he said. "Please form a single line. Helmets on. No running. The grinder is a scheduled maintenance window, and the maw is off-limits during feeding hours, which are posted." "Who are you?" the party leader said. "I'm the contractor," the man said. "The guild leased this floor. I fixed the structural integrity, negotiated a non-aggression pact with the maw, and I'm halfway through a cost-benefit analysis on the loot distribution. There's tea in the back room." The party stood in the entrance, weapons drawn, utterly defeated by efficiency. The grinder, for the first time in four hundred years, had a safety inspection certificate.

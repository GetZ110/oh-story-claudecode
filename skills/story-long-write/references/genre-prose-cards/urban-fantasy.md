# Urban Fantasy Prose Card

## Prose directive
Urban fantasy keeps two worlds colliding on every page: the ordinary city (rent, buses, diners, crime scenes) and the hidden one (wards, contracts, creatures, courts). The prose must make the magic feel like intrusion — something that smells, costs, and leaves marks — never like wallpaper behind the mundane. The genre voice is observational and slightly wry: the protagonist notices the hidden world in the texture of the visible one, and the reader trusts their eye.

## Opening hooks
- Cut in at the breach: a body the police will misread, a door that should not open onto that street, a client with a problem that isn't human.
- First scene establishes the protagonist's place in both worlds — and what they owe each of them.
- The mundane job (bartender, detective, courier, nurse) is the anchor; the case arrives through it.

## Conflict engine
Two-world politics + debts of blood and favor + a mundane life that keeps getting collateral damage. Conflict runs on what the hidden world costs the protagonist's humanity, family, or paycheck.

## Payoff & emotion release
Release comes from the protagonist out-knowing the supernatural with mundane tools, a bargain turned on its maker, the hidden world forced to honor a human promise, and protectors showing up in public. Release on visible proofs: a ward that finally holds, a name paid back, a body identified by a coffee stain.

## Dialogue & voice
Supernatural beings speak old and precise — contracts, not conversation. The protagonist speaks contemporary and blunt; their plainness is the counterweight that wins. Banter carries the tension; no one explains the lore they already know.

## Chapter-end hooks
Use a ward failing, a debt coming due, a creature recognizing the protagonist, a mundane witness left alive, a name spoken in the wrong court, or the other world arriving at the protagonist's workplace. The hook pulls both worlds tighter.

## Scene granularity
The texture lands on wards scratched into doorframes, cold iron, name contracts, blood ledger pages, the diner booth where both worlds meet, bus routes that don't appear on maps, and the mundane objects that become weapons (a fire extinguisher, a parking receipt, a belt). Realism comes from the ordinary being used against the mythic.

## Prose landing points
Open on the breach in the ordinary; conflict lands on two-world debts and collateral; endings land on a visible supernatural act with a mundane cost — or a human act that makes the supernatural blink.

## Early/mid/late play
- Early: establish the protagonist's foot in both worlds and the debt that keeps them there.
- Mid: alternate case-of-the-arc with world politics; every secret revealed raises a new price.
- Late: force the two worlds to meet in public; the ending settles the protagonist's citizenship — which world they belong to, by choice.

## Pacing density
One supernatural intrusion into the mundane per chapter minimum. No chapter of pure lore; the hidden world must keep touching the visible one.

## This-chapter tradeoffs
Pick one supernatural element and one mundane anchor per chapter; make each pay for the other.

## Drift bans
No lore dumps that stop the scene; no magic that solves mundane problems for free; no hidden world so hidden it never touches the city; no protagonist whose ordinary life evaporates once the magic starts.

## Sample passages

> 1. The body was found behind the diner at 4 a.m., and the officer on scene was already writing it up as a mugging — the wallet was gone, the phone was gone, and the wounds looked like a knife that had been used too many times. Dana looked at the man's hands instead. Both palms, clean. A man who'd been robbed in an alley in this neighborhood would have scraped palms from the asphalt. She knelt and checked the inside of his wrist: three pale lines, old, like someone had counted something on him three times. She'd seen those marks once, in a case file that didn't officially exist. She stood up. "It's not a mugging," she told the officer. "It's a payment."

> 2. The contract was nine pages long and written in a language that looked like old bank ledgers. Marcus had learned to read it the hard way — three years, one favor at a time. The clause he was looking for was on page seven, and it had taken him six months to find the loophole in it. He slid the paper across the counter. "Your client owes me a debt." The creature on the other stool didn't look at the paper. "Impossible. The debt was settled." "Settled in cash," Marcus said. "The contract says 'in kind.'" The creature finally picked up the paper. Its eyes moved down the page, stopped, and went very still. "You found the printer's mark." "I noticed it," Marcus said. "I've been noticing things for three years."

> 3. The ward on her apartment door had been scratched there by a previous tenant, and the landlord had painted over it twice. It still worked — barely — which was how she knew something was outside at 2 a.m., because the paint was humming. She didn't open the door. She didn't call anyone. She took the parking receipt off the counter, wrote her name and the date on it, and slid it under the door. Whatever was out there stopped humming. In the morning the receipt was gone, and the ward had been re-scratched, neatly, in fresh paint over the old lines. Someone had fixed it. Someone wanted her to know they could.

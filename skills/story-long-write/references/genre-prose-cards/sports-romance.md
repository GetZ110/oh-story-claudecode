# Sports Romance Prose Card

## Prose directive
The sport is the container; the rivalry is the engine. Every chapter carries a sport beat (game, practice, press, trade) that mirrors the relationship beat — a shift in the standings lands like a shift in trust. The athlete's life creates the obstacles: the trade that separates them, the press conference that lies, the injury that reveals care, the contract that sets a deadline. Banter is competitive; feelings arrive through the sport's vocabulary. Dual POV is the convention (both leads, both competent, both stubborn); the season structure — regular season, playoffs, final — is the plot clock. The rivalry is the attraction, never a wall to tear down; its dissolution is the romance's progress and the ending must not erase the competitive edge that made the couple work.

## Opening hooks
- Cut in at a game, a trade, a draft, a locker-room conflict, or a press scrum — the sport and the tension in one scene.
- First chapter establishes the rivalry's origin (the stolen position, the childhood game, the public insult, the hit that was "legal and personal") and the season's stakes.
- The first meeting is often the first collision — on the ice, the pitch, the track, or in the trade call that pairs them.

## Conflict engine
Rivalry + professional stakes + forced proximity (rivals-turned-teammates, shared facility, the trade that pairs them) + the season clock. Conflicts land on: the trade or transfer, the contract, the press, the playoff run, the injury, the teammates' loyalties, the family's claims. Misunderstandings come from the public life — a quote, a photo, an endorsement, a headline — and always have a real source and a real professional cost.

## Payoff & emotion release
Release comes from the rivalry's first genuine compliment, the public defense (the press conference where one takes the other's side), the shared championship goal, the almost-caught date, the locker-room almost-moment, the injury scene where care leaks through the trash talk, and the final game where the public choice lands. Each payoff lands twice: once private, once public — the genre's signature echo.

## Dialogue & voice
Competitive banter with subtext; trash talk that is actually flirting; short lines, quick volleys, one-liners that land. Locker-room register is coarse and affectionate; private register is vulnerable in short bursts. The press register is careful and on-record; the confession register is flat and factual. Dialogue share runs high — this genre breathes through exchanges.

## Chapter-end hooks
Use the trade announcement, the playoff bracket, the injury report, the press leak, the almost-caught moment, the rival's unexpected kindness, or the season countdown. The hook points at the next professional problem with a relationship cost.

## Scene granularity
The texture lands on the ice, pitch, or track; locker rooms, press rooms, team buses, training facilities, playoff hotels, contract negotiations, the transfer window, and the fan's-eye view (jerseys, chants, the arena's held breath). The sport's mechanics appear through consequence — a shift change, a penalty, a qualifying lap, a set point — never as broadcast filler.

## Prose landing points
Open on the collision (game, trade, press); conflict lands on the professional stakes and the rivalry's pushback; endings land on the season's result and the relationship's result in the same scene — the trophy and the ring, the contract and the confession.

## Early/mid/late play
- Early: the rivalry's origin, the season's stakes, the first forced proximity; banter establishes the attraction's resistance; each lead's professional flaw shown.
- Mid: the trade, transfer, or playoff run raises the stakes; one public misunderstanding with a real source; the first true vulnerability scene (the injury, the family, the fear of not making it).
- Late: the championship or final match; the public choice; HEA with the professional reality resolved — the trade back, the shared contract, the ring, the next season together.

## Pacing density
One sport beat per chapter mirroring one relationship beat; a big public scene every 3-5 chapters; the season arc carries the escalation. No consecutive chapters without a relationship-state change; no game scenes that do not move the relationship.

## This-chapter tradeoffs
The sport beat and the romance beat must move the same distance in the same chapter; one dominates, the other serves. If the game runs long, the relationship moves short — a glance, a line, a decision — but it moves.

## Drift bans
No game-filler (play-by-play with no relationship consequence); no manufactured press miscommunication (the quote, photo, or leak must have a real source); no athlete whose sport has no cost (injuries, trades, contracts, and retirement must matter); no rivalry that dissolves instantly (the competitive edge is the attraction — it survives the HEA); no HEA that ignores the professional reality; no cardboard teammates (the locker room must have its own voices and loyalties).

## Sample passages

> 1. The trade dropped at four in the morning, and by seven the press had decided it was a punishment. Noah read the headline on the bus — "Bad Blood Finally Cuts Its Loose End" — and then he read the text from the one person who should never have texted him: "You're on my flight. Gate 14. Don't make it weird." The trade had sent him to the same franchise as the man who had broken his collarbone in the playoffs, on purpose, in front of twenty thousand people. Noah looked at the text. It was going to be weird. But the season had thirty games left, and he was too old to start over anywhere else.

> 2. The press conference was supposed to be about the goal. It became about the assist, and the assist's owner, because the reporter asked the question everyone in the room had been pretending not to think about: "Coach says you two are starting to click. Any comment on the history?" Noah looked at the microphone, then at Marcus, who was watching him from the other end of the table with the expression of a man enjoying a free show. "The history," Noah said, "is that I hit him hard, he hit me harder, and we're both still standing." A laugh went through the room. "And now?" the reporter said. Noah thought about the assist, the way Marcus had known where he would be without looking. "And now," he said, "I'd rather have him on my side of the ice than against it." The room laughed again, and Marcus didn't, and Noah understood, too late, that he had just said the truest thing he had said all season in front of forty cameras.

> 3. The injury happened at home, in front of their own crowd, and the silence was the worst part — the building going quiet at once, the way it does when a body stays down. Marcus was the first one there, because of course he was, because the rivalry had a standing order: nobody else gets to hurt him. "Don't move," Marcus said, crouching beside him. "I'm not moving," Noah said. His knee was the wrong shape, and his season was probably over, and Marcus was holding his hand in front of forty thousand people, and no one was booing. "You're going to be fine," Marcus said. "I've studied this knee for years. I know it better than you do." Noah laughed, which hurt, which was worth it. "Weirdest thing you've ever said to me," he said. "Also the nicest." The season was over, and the playoffs were a rumor, and the story they had spent five years building between them was suddenly not about hockey at all.

> 4. The final was tied at the end of regulation, and the arena was a single held breath. Noah watched from the bench with his knee in ice and his heart in his throat as Marcus took the face-off, and he thought about the trade that had brought them together, and the press conference, and the hand in front of forty thousand people. Marcus won the face-off, passed, cut, and the puck came back to him at the blue line, and Noah was on his feet with the ice pack forgotten, screaming something that was not a word. The puck went in. The horn went off. The bench emptied onto the ice, and Marcus came straight through the pile, straight to the boards, and pointed at him — pointed, in front of everything, in front of the cameras and the crowd and the whole season — and mouthed the two words that had been the joke, the rivalry, and now, apparently, the whole truth: "Your turn." Noah looked at the ring box in his own pocket, bought the week of the trade, and understood that the story had always been going to end this way, on the ice, in front of everyone. He pulled himself over the boards on one leg and limped toward the only teammate who had ever been the enemy, which was, it turned out, the same thing as the only teammate who had ever been home.
